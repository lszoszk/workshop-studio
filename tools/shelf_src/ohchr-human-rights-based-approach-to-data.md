## Summary written for the workshop

**Purpose.** The note gives general guidance on a human rights-based approach to data (HRBAD), with a focus on how data are collected and disaggregated. It is written in the context of the 2030 Agenda and its pledge to leave no one behind, which called for more systematic disaggregation of data. The note stresses that deciding how to disaggregate is not a neutral technical choice: it can help reveal inequalities but can also put the people described in the data at risk. It is addressed to policymakers, statisticians and other data specialists in government and civil society, development practitioners and human rights advocates. It organises its guidance under six headings.

**1. Participation.** The people a dataset describes should have a real say in planning, collecting, analysing and publishing it, especially marginalised groups. Key points: use varied ways of involving people; say openly how participation was organised and what came of it; make sure the views of vulnerable or at-risk groups are represented; keep a record of what participatory work produced. The note admits that direct engagement is sometimes unsafe or inappropriate, for example where a group's legal status or social stigma exposes it to risk.

**2. Data disaggregation.** Breaking data down by group lets users compare groups and see disparities that national averages hide. Data should be disaggregated by the characteristics recognised in international human rights law; the note lists sex, age, ethnicity, migration or displacement status, disability, religion, civil status, income, and sexual orientation and gender identity. Where possible, data should allow analysis of intersecting disparities (for example gender and disability together). Collecting data that can be disaggregated may require different sampling designs, and the note calls birth registration foundational. Qualitative indicators and context (legal, institutional, cultural) are needed alongside the numbers.

**3. Self-identification.** Groups of interest should define themselves. Individuals should be able to choose whether to disclose personal characteristics, and information about them should come from the individuals, at their discretion. Collection must follow the principle of doing no harm, and designers should remember that the categories they choose carry norms and values.

**4. Transparency.** Data collectors should publish clear, accessible information about their operations, research design and methods, and official statistics are part of the public's right to information. Information on how data were collected should be public and data should be released as quickly as possible after collection.

**5. Privacy.** Privacy and confidentiality must be weighed together with access to information. Dissemination must not make individuals identifiable or reveal their personal characteristics; data collectors need robust protection mechanisms; personal data should be released only with the permission of the person concerned or their representatives.

**6. Accountability.** Data should be usable to hold States and other actors to account for human rights. Those who collect data are themselves accountable: national statistical offices are duty-bearers with obligations to respect, protect and fulfil human rights. Collectors are also responsible for the effects of what they publish, including the effect of making already-public information more visible or accessible, which can expose the people it describes or the people who gathered it.

## Notes for data apps built in the workshop

- Dashboards that show only national totals can hide the groups the note is concerned with. Plan which breakdowns the source data can support, and say plainly when a breakdown is missing.
- Small groups in a breakdown can identify individuals. Before publishing a disaggregated view, check cell sizes and decide on a suppression or aggregation rule; this connects the disaggregation and privacy headings.
- Document how the data were collected and what categories mean (see the Turing Way page on data documentation), which serves the transparency heading.
- Do not paste personal or sensitive records into a public AI tool to analyse them; see the ICO and Analysis Function pages on this shelf.
