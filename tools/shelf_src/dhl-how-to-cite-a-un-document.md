## Summary written for the workshop

**What the Library says.** There is no single prescribed way to cite a source. For United Nations documents, publications and websites, the Library recommends that a citation include as many of the following elements as apply:

- the title;
- the publication date;
- the unique identifiers: the UN document symbol and, for sales publications, the UN sales number;
- the URL, together with the date on which you accessed it;
- page or paragraph numbers for the passage you rely on;
- the creator or author, which for many items is the United Nations itself.

**When the type of item is unclear.** If you cannot tell what kind of material you have, and you found it online, the Library suggests citing it as a website.

**Which style to use.** The Library says it does not specialise in citation styles. As part of the UN Secretariat it points UN staff and delegates to the United Nations Editorial Manual, and it links the manual's chapter on footnotes and other references. It notes that the styles differ mostly in presentation: the order of the elements, capitalisation, punctuation, how the author's name is written, and how references appear in the running text. The Bluebook, a US legal citation manual, is described as the main English-language guide that deals with UN documents, and many other guides refer back to it. Researchers, students and authors from outside the UN are told to consult the manual of the style their university, teacher or publisher requires, or to ask a librarian or writing centre.

**General advice.** Citing UN documents can be hard, so give as much detail as possible. UN materials usually have distinctive identifiers, above all the document symbol, that let a reader find the exact item.

**Related Library guides named in the entry.** A research guide on UN document symbols and series symbols (summarised in the next page on this shelf), the United Nations Editorial Manual, and the Bluebook.

## What this means for a citation-finder app

- Store the **fields** separately (symbol, title, date, creator, URL, access date, paragraph or page) and format them at the end according to the style the user chooses. The Library's point that styles differ mainly in ordering and punctuation supports this design.
- Treat the **document symbol** as the primary key for a UN document: it is what identifies the item across languages, sessions and websites.
- Record the **access date** when the source is a web page, and keep a **pinpoint** (paragraph or page) because the Library lists it as part of a good citation.
- Make the app say which style it applied, and let the user override it; the Library does not claim that one style is correct for everyone.
- Never let the app invent a symbol or URL. If a field is unknown, leave it empty and flag it.
