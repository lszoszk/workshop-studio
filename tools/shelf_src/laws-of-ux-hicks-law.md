## Summary written for the workshop

**The idea.** Hick's law says that people take longer to decide as the number of options, or the complexity of the options, goes up. The page presents it as a rule of thumb for interface design: every extra choice you put in front of someone costs them decision time and mental effort.

**Where it comes from.** The law is named after the British psychologist William Edmund Hick and the American psychologist Ray Hyman. According to the page, they studied in 1952 how reaction time relates to the number of possible stimuli: the more alternatives, the slower the response.

**Takeaways listed on the page, in short form:**

- Where quick responses matter, cut down the number of options.
- Break a complicated task into smaller steps so that each step asks for less.
- Do not leave users to weigh everything alone: mark the recommended option.
- Introduce features gradually for new users (progressive onboarding) instead of showing everything at once.
- Do not over-simplify to the point that the interface becomes vague or abstract. Fewer choices is not the same as less clarity.

**Examples the page uses.** The Google search page keeps the decision to a minimum by leaving out content that would distract from typing a query. The Apple TV remote moves complexity out of the physical device and into on-screen menus that reveal information step by step. Slack's onboarding starts new users with a single capability, messaging, and brings in further features later.

**The page gives no numeric limit** on how many options are acceptable; it states a direction (fewer and simpler choices decide faster), not a threshold.

## Using it when planning a small data app

- A dashboard with twelve filters on first load asks for twelve decisions. Start with the two or three most used filters and place the rest behind a clearly labelled "More filters" control.
- A long menu of document types can be grouped into a few headings, or replaced by a search box with suggestions, so people choose among a handful of groups first.
- For a multi-step tool (upload a PDF, check the extracted table, export), show one step at a time and mark the usual next action as the main button.
- Offer a sensible default for settings that most people will not change, and say what it is.
- If you remove options, check that experts who need them can still reach them (compare the "flexibility and efficiency" heuristic in the Nielsen Norman Group summary on this shelf).
