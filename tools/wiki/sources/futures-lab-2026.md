# Futures Lab 2026: Neurotechnology and the Administration of Justice (submission to OHCHR)

**Full cite.** Futures Lab for Democracy, Tech & Human Rights, American University, *Neurotechnology and the Administration of Justice: Submission to the Office of the UN High Commissioner for Human Rights for Input to the Report of the UN Secretary-General Pursuant to UN General Assembly Resolution A/RES/79/172*, 9 March 2026, 8 printed pages (printed page = PDF page minus 1), submitted to OHCHR's call for input, https://www.ohchr.org/en/calls-for-input/2026/call-input-human-rights-administration-justice-neurotechnology-and-other. Contributors: Professor Jonathan Crock and twenty named co-contributors [Futures Lab 2026, p. 1](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p2 "Submitted by: Futures Lab for Democracy, Tech & Human Rights, American University Contributors: Professor Jonathan Crock, Joy Adjei") ✓.

**Register.** (e): civil society. An advocacy submission by a university lab, written as input to the Secretary-General's report ([[a-81-351]]); neither the text nor the footnotes of A/81/351 name it. Its weight is that of its argument and sources, which the library cannot check.

**One-liner.** A civil-society case for new UN standards on neurotechnology, framed in UDHR terms ("neurorights", "cognitive liberty", "sovereignty over one's brain"), with US state laws, Girardi v. Emotiv and discrimination risks as its evidence.

## Key takeaways

**New standards.** The central ask [Futures Lab 2026, p. 7](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p8 "the United Nations needs to develop new human rights standards that reflect the emerging and rapidly evolving nature of neurotechnology") ✓, stated also in the summary [Futures Lab 2026, p. 2](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p3 "the United Nations should establish global standards grounded in human dignity, cognitive liberty, and equality") ✓ and for data [Futures Lab 2026, p. 4](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p5 "specific international standards must be established to recognize the highly sensitive and personal nature of bio- and neurotechnologies") ✓. Until then, monitoring [Futures Lab 2026, p. 7](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p8 "Until such standards are established, the use of neurotechnology, particularly in legal responses to violations of neurorights, should be subject to careful monitoring") ✓. This is the library's clearest "new rights" pole: [[a-81-351]] records both views without choosing (para. 16) and [[ahrc-2025]] reads the debate as settling for existing rights (p. 30).

**UDHR framing.** [Futures Lab 2026, p. 4](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p5 "Neurorights safeguard fundamental aspects of human dignity, ensuring that mental processes and cognitive functions remain inviolable. They align with Articles 12 and 18 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR)") ✓. The submission never cites the Covenant, article 14, the Human Rights Committee or a General Comment; its other instrument is UNESCO's bioethics declaration [Futures Lab 2026, p. 4](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p5 "Although UNESCO’s Universal Declaration on Bioethics and Human Rights in Article 6 mandates") ✓. It asks for a right no UN document in the library names [Futures Lab 2026, p. 4](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p5 "stricter legal protections must be enacted to protect the human right to sovereignty over one’s brain") ✓.

**US state laws.** [Futures Lab 2026, p. 4](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p5 "California and Colorado are currently the only states to have passed legislation on neural data privacy, while a dozen others have legislation pending") ✓, with California's definition [Futures Lab 2026, p. 4](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p5 "information that is generated by measuring the activity of a consumer’s central or peripheral nervous system, and that is not inferred from nonneural information") ✓. "Only" is contradicted three times: [[a-81-351]] (para. 45) and [[szoszkiewicz-yuste-2025]] (p. 3471) add Montana, and the submission itself later says [Futures Lab 2026, p. 8](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p9 "At least eight U.S. states have enacted legislation to protect neurorights") ✓. Treat its count as unreliable; keep its point that no federal standard exists [Futures Lab 2026, p. 2](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p3 "yet no federal standard exists") ✓.

**Girardi v. Emotiv.** The remedies [Futures Lab 2026, p. 6](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p7 "The Chilean Supreme Court ruled that Emotiv had sold the device without adequate oversight of health and human rights, ordering the deletion of collected neural data and the suspension of sales") ✓ and the lesson drawn [Futures Lab 2026, p. 6](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p7 "commercialization cannot be justified solely by click-through consent and emphasizes the need to institutionalize HRIAs as a pre-market requirement") ✓. [[a-81-351]] (para. 43) describes the same judgment as holding the company's practices unlawful under the 2021 constitutional amendment, without the remedies.

**Consent.** [Futures Lab 2026, p. 7](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p8 "Individuals should not be able to consent to overly broad uses of neurotechnology until clear and established neurorights are in place") ✓: a limit on consent itself, not only on its conditions.

**Participation.** Regulation is top-down and excludes those affected [Futures Lab 2026, p. 3](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p4 "Those directly impacted by neurotechnology, including defendants and prisoners … are regarded as secondary advisors or “objects” in the regulatory process") ✓; France is its example [Futures Lab 2026, p. 3](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p4 "France was the first country to allow brain imaging in judicial proceedings with consent, for medical or scientific research, or for forensic expertise, except for functional brain imaging") ✓. The same section sees promise in neuroprediction [Futures Lab 2026, p. 3](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p4 "While there is promise in neurotechnology’s assistance in assessing guilt, accountability, and the risk of recidivism in judicial proceedings") ✓: the only library document to say so; [[a-81-351]] (paras. 10, 33–35) and [[ahrc-2025]] treat recidivism prediction as a threat.

**Discrimination.** Biased datasets and people of African descent [Futures Lab 2026, p. 6](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p7 "The training data for these algorithms is often not diverse and draws on historical records, making outputs less accurate and potentially harmful to people of African descent") ✓; LGBTQI+ persons and "involuntary outing" [Futures Lab 2026, p. 7](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p8 "may infer sexual orientation or gender identity based on neural responses to stimuli, raising the possibility of involuntary outing in workplaces, medical contexts, or legal systems") ✓.

## How NOT to read this

- Advocacy, register (e): "needs", "must" and "should" are the authors' demands, not law, and "neurorights" or "cognitive liberty" are their vocabulary. The UN documents in the library use "neurorights" only as the name of domestic instruments ([[a-81-351]], para. 43).
- UDHR, not ICCPR: the rights map stops at the Declaration. For the Covenant, use [[iccpr-art-17]], [[iccpr-art-18]], [[iccpr-art-14-3-g]].
- Page 5 (PDF p. 6) calls UNESCO's standards a "draft" ("Draft global standards", "The UNESCO draft"); [[a-81-351]] (para. 46) records their adoption in 2025, before this March 2026 submission. See [[unesco-recommendation-2025]].
- The words it attributes to article 6 of the Universal Declaration on Bioethics and Human Rights cannot be checked in the library; quote that Declaration, not the submission.
- Section 10, "Judicial Decisions" (p. 8), is about AI in hiring (Amazon, Mobley v. Workday), not neurotechnology or the administration of justice.
- Its technical claims (79 per cent decoding accuracy, Neuralink's "21 clinical patients", NeuroXess, Kampto) are unverified in the library.
