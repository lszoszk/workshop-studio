# UNODC–INTERPOL 2025: Neurotechnology, Law Enforcement and Criminal Justice

**Full cite.** United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime (UNODC) and International Criminal Police Organization (INTERPOL), *Neurotechnology, Law Enforcement and Criminal Justice: background paper* (United Nations, October 2025; "© United Nations, October 2025. All rights reserved."), 8 printed pages, https://www.interpol.int/How-we-work/Innovation/INTERPOL-UNODC-Neurotechnology-assessment. The PDF has 11 pages; a printed page is the PDF page minus 3, and pin labels carry the printed page. The paper calls itself "preliminary thoughts" [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 3](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p6 "This background paper shares some preliminary thoughts on the field of neurotechnology and its significance for law enforcement and the criminal justice system, focusing on opportunities, risks, and human rights safeguards") ✓, opens a consultation towards a Technology Assessment [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 6](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p9 "Building on this background paper, a Technology Assessment will be developed to provide more comprehensive guidance") ✓ and dates that document's presentation [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 8](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p11 "The final version of the Technology Assessment will be presented during the Fifteenth United Nations Congress on Crime Prevention and Criminal Justice which will be held from 25 to 30 April 2026 in Abu Dhabi") ✓. Only this public paper is in the library.

**Register.** (c): a UN agency paper, written with a police organisation for a consultation. It is the only library document written from the law-enforcement side: it claims a "pivotal role" in guiding police [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 3](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p6 "UNODC and INTERPOL have a pivotal role to play in facilitating global dialogue, and in guiding law enforcement agencies and criminal justice institutions toward a responsible stance") ✓. It cites the Advisory Committee and the Special Rapporteur on the right to privacy; it makes no treaty analysis of its own.

**One-liner.** A consultation paper that lists what police and courts might do with neurotechnology, the rights at stake and the safeguards that must come first, then asks the field for evidence.

## Key takeaways

**Definition.** Neural data is [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 1](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p4 "information obtained by measuring the activity of a person's central or peripheral nervous system") ✓. Compare the UNESCO definition adopted in [[a-81-351]] (para. 5) and California's statutory one reported in [[futures-lab-2026]] (p. 4): the library holds three definitions, not one.

**Uses in view.** Lie detection and risk assessment first [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 1](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p4 "they are increasingly considered for use in criminal investigations for lie detection and risk assessments") ✓; then a wider list, including officer monitoring, with a readiness caveat [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 3](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p6 "Potential uses include memory detection, risk assessment, the decoding of thoughts in the form of words, or neuroenhancement … these use cases vary significantly in their level of scientific validity and technical readiness") ✓. Reintegration uses (neurofeedback, brain stimulation for violent offenders) rest on studies the paper itself calls preliminary [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 2](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p5 "many of these studies are preliminary") ✓.

**The test it proposes.** Benefit, then necessity and proportionality [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 3](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p6 "A demonstrable and significant benefit for law enforcement functions must also be shown, and the necessity and proportionality of their use assessed against the potential human rights impacts") ✓.

**Rights at stake.** Brain activity is "among the most intimate forms of personal information" and monitoring it "without genuine consent would represent a profound intrusion" [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 4](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p7 "Brain activity is among the most intimate forms of personal information … without genuine consent would represent a profound intrusion into human dignity, autonomy, and privacy") ✓. Due process is listed by name [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 4](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p7 "Due process rights are also at stake, including the right to appear before a competent judicial authority, the presumption of innocence, the privilege against self-incrimination, and the right to appeal") ✓, and so is the "offered" use that is coercive in effect [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 4](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p7 "under conditions in which individuals are “offered” the use of these technologies in ways that are coercive, or under the guise of moral-enhancement") ✓. Discrimination is "a key concern", relayed from the Advisory Committee [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 4](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p7 "the potential for the discriminatory use of neurotechnologies … is a key concern") ✓. Governance must track the treaties "as interpreted by treaty monitoring bodies" [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 4](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p7 "clear governance frameworks that align innovation with human rights protection as established in human rights treaties and as interpreted by treaty monitoring bodies") ✓.

**Evidence.** Accuracy is open and admissibility case by case [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 4](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p7 "questions remain about the accuracy of technologies that seek to detect memory or deception. While there is no standardized approach to this globally, courts in some jurisdictions have assessed the admissibility of neuroimaging evidence on a case-by-case basis") ✓. The paper asks, without answering [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 5](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p8 "Can courts legally order a criminal defendant, even in narrow circumstances, to cooperate with law enforcement in the use of neurotechnologies?") ✓.

**Safeguards.** A precondition of deployment [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 5](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p8 "Technologies should therefore not be deployed until their accuracy, safety, and feasibility have been rigorously demonstrated through independent testing and peer-reviewed research") ✓; control over neural data and judicial oversight [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 6](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p9 "Individuals must retain control over their neural data, and any interference with mental integrity or cognitive processes must be strictly limited, lawful, and subject to judicial oversight") ✓; consent in custody [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 6](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p9 "Consent must be voluntary and informed, free of coercion or undue influence, particularly in custodial or correctional settings where power imbalances are acute") ✓; rules on admissibility and oversight [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 6](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p9 "developing clear rules on admissibility of evidence, establishing independent mechanisms for oversight and accountability") ✓; harmonisation across borders [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 6](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p9 "international cooperation and harmonization of standards will be essential") ✓.

**Criminal misuse.** The paper's distinctive angle: attacks on BCIs and neural data as new crimes [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 5](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p8 "Unauthorized access to BCIs, manipulation of neural data, or the use of neurotechnologies for covert communication could present new and unprecedented threats") ✓, with "few legal frameworks in place to criminalize" them [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 3](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p6 "there are currently few legal frameworks in place to criminalize the malicious or irresponsible use of neurotechnologies") ✓.

## How NOT to read this

- Not the Technology Assessment. The paper announces that document; nothing in the library says what it contains, and this page says nothing about it.
- A law-enforcement perspective. It starts from "opportunities" and a "demonstrable and significant benefit" test; the Secretary-General starts from rights and recommends prohibiting coercive use ([[a-81-351]], para. 50 (c)); AHRC proposes a moratorium ([[ahrc-2025]]). Its "not be deployed until" is a validation condition, not a moratorium.
- "They have also been used to decode human thought in the form of words into text on a screen" (p. 1, PDF p. 4) describes research with consenting participants. [[a-81-351]] says neurotechnology "is not capable at present of mind-reading" (para. 22) and that decoding's reliability for justice is unknown (para. 11).
- Pages 7–8 are a call for submissions (questions, areas of focus, a deadline of 10 November 2025): a questionnaire, not findings.
- Printed page = PDF page minus 3. The pin labels are right; a reader paging the PDF by hand must subtract.
