# A/81/351: Human rights in the administration of justice (Secretary-General, 2026)

**Full cite.** United Nations General Assembly, *Human rights in the administration of justice: report of the Secretary-General*, UN Doc. A/81/351, eighty-first session, agenda item 70 (b), 27 August 2026, 16 pages, https://docs.un.org/en/A/81/351. Prepared under General Assembly resolution 79/172 from submissions and research [A/81/351, para. 1](pin:A/81/351#p2 "submitted pursuant to General Assembly resolution 79/172 … The report draws on information received from Member States, United Nations entities, human rights mechanisms and civil society and on external research") ✓. Fifty paragraphs; the recommendations are para. 50 (a)–(j).

**Register.** (c): a report of the Secretary-General to the General Assembly, synthesising submissions, Special Procedures reports and the Committee's General Comments. Where it quotes the Committee, pin the General Comment ([[ccpr-c-gc-32]], [[general-comment-22]]); cite this report for the application to neurotechnology.

**One-liner.** The latest UN-level map of neurotechnology in the administration of justice: it names the uses, runs them through the Covenant rights, surveys regulation and recommends that Member States prohibit compulsory or coercive use.

## Key takeaways

**Neural data.** The definition comes from the UNESCO Recommendation (its footnote 6, on the same PDF page), and the report states a consensus [A/81/351, para. 5](pin:A/81/351#p3 "There is broad consensus that neural data are highly sensitive, providing unprecedented access to internal mental states, and that they require heightened ethical and legal protection") ✓. Its footnote 7, on the same PDF page, cites A/HRC/58/58, Szoszkiewicz and Yuste 2025 (the EMBO Reports article in the library, [[szoszkiewicz-yuste-2025]]) and Genser, Damianos and Yuste 2024: the consensus is sourced to those three works. The conclusion repeats it [A/81/351, para. 49](pin:A/81/351#p15 "Neural data, as uniquely sensitive personal data, demand heightened protection") ✓.

**Uses.** "Brain-based deception detection" is the umbrella term; fMRI "lie detection" and concealed-information tests sold as "brain fingerprinting" are its species [A/81/351, para. 8](pin:A/81/351#p4 "The most discussed application is the use of functional magnetic resonance imaging for “lie detection”. Other related but distinct techniques are “guilty knowledge” or “concealed information” testing") ✓. Both terms recur: "deception detection" in paras. 8, 9, 20 and 50 (c), "lie detection" in paras. 8, 9, 28 and 32. None is court-ready [A/81/351, para. 9](pin:A/81/351#p4 "No brain-based deception detection method has achieved general acceptance for courtroom use") ✓. Whether decoding will ever be reliable enough for justice "is unknown" [A/81/351, para. 11](pin:A/81/351#p4 "Whether such approaches will, at some point, be able to produce information that is sufficiently reliable to be used in the administration of justice is unknown") ✓.

**New law or existing law.** Both positions are recorded, neither chosen [A/81/351, para. 16](pin:A/81/351#p6 "While some contend that the challenges of neurotechnology may require development of new legal frameworks, others affirm that the risks of misuse or abuse of neurotechnology may be addressed within the existing international human rights law framework") ✓. The conclusion leans to the second, with a caveat [A/81/351, para. 48](pin:A/81/351#p15 "International human rights law provides an applicable framework to address the risks of neurotechnology, but further clarification and consistent application are needed") ✓.

**Freedom of thought.** Applying General Comment No. 22 (its footnote 36, on the same PDF page), the report finds that deception detection and decoding "could reveal information that the person has not chosen to disclose" [A/81/351, para. 20](pin:A/81/351#p7 "through brain-based deception detection technology or decoding, could reveal information that the person has not chosen to disclose") ✓. The four pillars are attributed to experts, not to the Committee [A/81/351, para. 21](pin:A/81/351#p7 "Experts have argued that four pillars of freedom of thought are most relevant to neurotechnology: the freedom not to disclose thoughts; freedom from punishment for thoughts, real or inferred; freedom from impermissible alteration of thoughts; and an enabling environment for free thought") ✓. All four "may potentially" be affected, though the report denies mind-reading [A/81/351, para. 22](pin:A/81/351#p8 "Neurotechnology may potentially affect all four pillars of freedom of thought … While neurotechnology is not capable at present of “mind-reading”, it is reportedly capable of inferring emotions and internal reactions based on neural data") ✓.

**Neuromodulation of offenders and consent.** The report relays the Advisory Committee's view with its hedges intact [A/81/351, para. 29](pin:A/81/351#p10 "The Human Rights Council Advisory Committee is of the view that the use of neuromodulation techniques intended to alter the brain activity of offenders to reduce aggression or impulsivity may constitute treatment contrary to articles 7 or 10 of the Covenant when imposed without genuinely free and informed consent") ✓ (the cited report, [[a-hrc-57-61]], is in the inbox), then turns to custody and early release [A/81/351, para. 29](pin:A/81/351#p10 "In custodial settings, the pronounced power imbalance between the State and the individual may call into question the genuineness of consent … early release or favourable parole outcomes, there is a significant risk that consent may not be fully free or voluntary") ✓.

**Fair trial, self-incrimination, liberty.** Coercive lie detection "could call into question" article 14 (3) (g); Selvi v. State of Karnataka is cited (its footnote 63, on PDF p. 11, gives paras. 215–224 of the judgment); the exclusionary rule "could apply" [A/81/351, para. 32](pin:A/81/351#p10 "The coercive use of brain-based lie detection could call into question the privilege against self-incrimination, protected under article 14 (3) (g) of the Covenant … The Supreme Court of India, in Selvi v. State of Karnataka, held that the involuntary administration of brain electrical oscillation signature profiling tests, a form of brain-based lie detection, violates the constitutional protection against self-incrimination … This could apply to evidence obtained through the coercive use of neurotechnologies") ✓. Prediction "may also undermine the presumption of innocence" [A/81/351, para. 33](pin:A/81/351#p11 "the use of predictive neurotechnology purporting to assess future criminality may also undermine the presumption of innocence") ✓ and "may lead to arbitrary detention under article 9" [A/81/351, para. 34](pin:A/81/351#p11 "the use of brain-based deception technologies to determine whether a person should be detained, may lead to arbitrary detention under article 9") ✓.

**Regulation.** "Nascent and uneven", citing A/HRC/57/61 [A/81/351, para. 42](pin:A/81/351#p13 "the regulatory response to the human rights challenges posed by neurotechnology is still nascent and uneven") ✓. Chile [A/81/351, para. 43](pin:A/81/351#p13 "In Chile, in 2023, the Supreme Court applied the 2021 constitutional amendment, to protect brain activity and derived data, holding unlawful a company’s practices involving a portable brain-monitoring device") ✓ (its footnote 75, on the same PDF page, names Girardi v. Emotiv Inc., 9 August 2023); three US states [A/81/351, para. 45](pin:A/81/351#p14 "California, Colorado and Montana have extended sensitive data protections to neural data") ✓ (its footnote 79, on the same PDF page: Colorado HB 24-1058, California SB 1223, Montana SB 163); UNESCO and the UNODC–INTERPOL collaboration [A/81/351, para. 46](pin:A/81/351#p14 "the General Conference of UNESCO adopted its Recommendation on the Ethics of Neurotechnology in 2025 … In 2025, the United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime and the International Criminal Police Organization launched a collaboration") ✓.

**What the Secretary-General recommends.** The verb is "recommend", the addressee Member States [A/81/351, para. 50](pin:A/81/351#p15 "I recommend that Member States:") ✓: frameworks with impact assessments [A/81/351, para. 50 (a)](pin:A/81/351#p15 "Establish regulatory frameworks, consistent with international human rights law") ✓; neural data as highly sensitive [A/81/351, para. 50 (b)](pin:A/81/351#p15 "Recognize neural data as highly sensitive personal data subject to the highest level of legal protection") ✓; prohibition of coercive use [A/81/351, para. 50 (c)](pin:A/81/351#p15 "Prohibit the compulsory or coercive use of neurotechnologies in the administration of justice, including brain-based deception detection, predictive applications, decoding applications to “read” thoughts, intentions or memories, neuromodulation and arbitrary or unlawful cognitive surveillance and monitoring") ✓; exclusion of tainted neural data [A/81/351, para. 50 (d)](pin:A/81/351#p15 "Prohibit the use of neural data obtained through torture or ill-treatment, or in violation of the rights to freedom of thought and opinion, as evidence in any proceedings") ✓; demanding consent [A/81/351, para. 50 (e)](pin:A/81/351#p15 "Require free, prior, informed, explicit and revocable consent … any consent should be subject to the highest evidentiary standards and rigorous scrutiny") ✓; validated evidence [A/81/351, para. 50 (f)](pin:A/81/351#p15 "Require rigorous independent scientific review and validation of any neurotechnology-derived evidence proposed for admission in court") ✓; safeguards for specific groups [A/81/351, para. 50 (g)](pin:A/81/351#p16 "Ensure enhanced safeguards for children, detainees, persons with disabilities, women, older persons") ✓ and Indigenous Peoples [A/81/351, para. 50 (h)](pin:A/81/351#p16 "the requirement of free, prior and informed consent") ✓; business due diligence [A/81/351, para. 50 (i)](pin:A/81/351#p16 "Urge all business enterprises involved in neurotechnology") ✓; training [A/81/351, para. 50 (j)](pin:A/81/351#p16 "specialized training for law enforcement officials, prosecutors, judges and defence counsel") ✓. No moratorium is recommended: para. 50 (c) prohibits coercion, paras. 50 (e)–(f) condition any other use.

## How NOT to read this

- A (c) report that synthesises submissions. Its verbs are "could", "may", "is of the view", "I recommend". Nothing here is a treaty-body finding; a recommendation to Member States is not an obligation.
- Para. 32 paraphrases General Comment No. 32 (its footnote 62, on the same PDF page) as "an absolute guarantee" against "any direct or indirect physical or psychological pressure". The Committee's words, pinned on [[ccpr-c-gc-32]] (para. 41), are "undue psychological pressure", and the Committee does not call article 14 (3) (g) absolute. Quote the Committee, not the paraphrase.
- Para. 5's "broad consensus" is a report citing three works (footnote 7), one of them scholarship in this library: a well-sourced claim, not a norm.
- Para. 29's view belongs to the Advisory Committee and to the "experts" of its footnote 56 (a UNODC submission and AHRC, Peace of Mind); the report adds no finding of its own.
- Footnote 42 (PDF p. 8) dates AHRC, Peace of Mind, to 2024; the library's copy is October 2025 ([[ahrc-2025]]).
- Paras. 42–46 report national positions from submissions; they are not findings of law.
