# AHRC 2025: Peace of Mind

**Full cite.** Australian Human Rights Commission, *Peace of Mind: Navigating the Ethical Frontiers of Neurotechnology and Human Rights* (Sydney: Australian Human Rights Commission, October 2025), CC BY 4.0, [publisher page](https://humanrights.gov.au/resource-hub/search-all-our-publications/reports-and-projects/reports/technology-and-human-rights/peace-of-mind-new-report-explores-neurotechnology-and-human-rights). The final report of a two-year project [AHRC 2025, p. 6](pin:AHRC-2025#p6 "this Final Report is the culmination of a multi-year project examining the human rights impacts of neurotechnology, it is not intended as the final word on this topic") ✓, with 18 recommendations [AHRC 2025, p. 9](pin:AHRC-2025#p9 "The final report makes 18 recommendations") ✓. Printed page = PDF page.

**Register.** (d): a national human rights institution addressing the Australian Government. Its Covenant readings are second-hand; chapters 5, 7 and 8 are largely Australian domestic law. Never cite it as (b): for any Covenant proposition go to the General Comment ([[general-comment-22]], [[ccpr-c-gc-32]], [[ccpr-c-gc-34]]) and use this report for what it adds.

**One-liner.** An NHRI records the neurorights debate as settling towards existing rights, asks the Human Rights Committee for a general comment on article 18, and takes the library's only institutional procedural position on criminal justice: an inquiry, a moratorium and interim inadmissibility.

## Key takeaways

**The debate (§6.3, p. 30).** [AHRC 2025, p. 30](pin:AHRC-2025#p30 "At the international and transnational level, these debates appear to be settling in favour of interpreting and adapting existing rights") ✓. An observation ("appear to be"), not a holding; the report then brackets the question [AHRC 2025, p. 30](pin:AHRC-2025#p30 "the broader discussion of neurorights is outside the scope of this report") ✓. None of its 18 recommendations proposes a new right.

**Freedom of thought (chapter 6, pp. 28–29).** Article 18 is the right most exposed, in the Committee's borrowed words [AHRC 2025, p. 28](pin:AHRC-2025#p28 "The right contained in art 18 ICCPR is ‘far reaching and profound’ and captures freedom of thought on all matters") ✓ (General Comment No. 22, para. 1: [[general-comment-22]]). The report then gives "four key pillars", attributed to the Special Rapporteur on freedom of religion or belief [AHRC 2025, p. 29](pin:AHRC-2025#p29 "freedom not to disclose thoughts … freedom from punishment for thoughts … freedom from impermissible alteration of thoughts … an enabling environment for freedom of thought") ✓. Its endnote 120 (PDF p. 77) cites A/76/380 (not in the library; [docs.un.org](https://docs.un.org/en/A/76/380)), where these are possible attributes; the AHRC hardens them into structure. The first pillar is read as mental privacy [AHRC 2025, p. 29](pin:AHRC-2025#p29 "mental privacy is a foundational element of free thought") ✓.

**Recommendation 4.** [AHRC 2025, p. 70](pin:AHRC-2025#p70 "The United Nations Human Rights Committee provide guidance on freedom of thought and neurotechnologies through the adoption of a general comment on article 18") ✓. First boxed on p. 28 (a box the passages do not carry; check by eye), repeated on p. 70.

**Chapter 7 is "initial observations" (p. 31).** [AHRC 2025, p. 31](pin:AHRC-2025#p31 "This chapter provides some initial observations of relevant risks and benefits and recommends that the Attorney-General request that the ALRC conduct an inquiry") ✓; meanwhile [AHRC 2025, p. 31](pin:AHRC-2025#p31 "it will be necessary to introduce a moratorium") ✓.

**Recommendation 6 and the preliminary view.** [AHRC 2025, p. 70](pin:AHRC-2025#p70 "examine in what circumstances (if any) it can be appropriately used. Until this work is completed there should be a moratorium on the use of neurotechnology in the criminal justice system") ✓ (boxed first on p. 31). The body text on p. 32 narrows the moratorium to investigations and adds interim inadmissibility [AHRC 2025, p. 32](pin:AHRC-2025#p32 "The Commission’s preliminary view is that a moratorium on the use of neurotechnology in criminal investigations should be imposed until the ALRC has published the findings of the recommended inquiry. Additionally, until that time, information gained via neurotechnology should be inadmissible at trial") ✓. Inadmissibility is body text, not part of the numbered recommendation.

**Lie detection and consent (7.1, pp. 31–32).** Brain-based lie detection is mapped onto article 14(3)(g) [AHRC 2025, p. 31](pin:AHRC-2025#p31 "there can be no direct or indirect physical or psychological pressure to secure a confession or testimony. The use of neurotechnology as a means of lie detection may threaten this human right") ✓ (the standard is General Comment No. 32, para. 41, whose "undue psychological pressure" the AHRC restates without "undue": [[ccpr-c-gc-32]]). The sharpest point is on inducements [AHRC 2025, p. 32](pin:AHRC-2025#p32 "if an accused was offered the opportunity for a reduced sentence or some level of immunity in exchange for consenting to the use of neurotechnology during questioning by police, it would be difficult to argue that any consent given was entirely voluntary") ✓, given [AHRC 2025, p. 32](pin:AHRC-2025#p32 "the imbalance of power in a criminal investigation") ✓. It adds a jury-role objection [AHRC 2025, p. 32](pin:AHRC-2025#p32 "neurotechnology’s ability to overtake a jury’s role in assessing witness credibility and determining truth") ✓.

**Memory recovery (7.2, p. 33).** TMS-assisted recall is placed under articles 17 and 18 [AHRC 2025, p. 33](pin:AHRC-2025#p33 "there is a real risk of breaching the right to privacy and freedom of thought as protected by arts 17 and 18 ICCPR") ✓, and forcible extraction under article 7, on the Advisory Committee's authority [AHRC 2025, p. 33](pin:AHRC-2025#p33 "The UN Human Rights Advisory Committee has observed that any misuse of the technology to this degree would represent a violation of art 7 ICCPR") ✓ (its endnote 170, PDF p. 79, cites A/HRC/57/61, paras. 34–35: [[a-hrc-57-61]]). Conclusion: [AHRC 2025, p. 33](pin:AHRC-2025#p33 "it should not be used in criminal investigations for memory recovery") ✓.

**Trial and mens rea (7.3, p. 34).** [AHRC 2025, p. 34](pin:AHRC-2025#p34 "Neurotechnology directly interferes with the offender’s mind, blurring the lines between sole responsibility and ‘third party’ involvement. This can have significant implications with respect to the application of current legal tests relating to criminal responsibility and establishing intention") ✓. Article 14 sets the limits [AHRC 2025, p. 34](pin:AHRC-2025#p34 "must not impede the right to be equal before the court and the presumption of innocence protected by art 14") ✓.

**Neuroprediction (7.4, pp. 34–35).** fMRI biomarkers as recidivism predictors [AHRC 2025, p. 34](pin:AHRC-2025#p34 "biological markers of human behaviours that correlate to behavioural traits … This is known as ‘neuroprediction’") ✓ are rejected [AHRC 2025, p. 35](pin:AHRC-2025#p35 "reminiscent of an outdated biological determinism that is an anathema to human rights") ✓, for discrimination [AHRC 2025, p. 35](pin:AHRC-2025#p35 "may breach human rights to be treated equally before the law and to non-discrimination") ✓ and self-fulfilling prophecy [AHRC 2025, p. 35](pin:AHRC-2025#p35 "may result in a self-fulfilling prophecy") ✓; the matter is routed to the inquiry [AHRC 2025, p. 35](pin:AHRC-2025#p35 "should only be used in criminal justice settings after specific research") ✓.

**Sentencing options (7.5, p. 35).** The custody problem in mirror image: [AHRC 2025, p. 35](pin:AHRC-2025#p35 "consent to the use of neurotechnology could be used transactionally for sentence reduction or an earlier parole – despite the offender’s preference not to be the subject of a neurointervention in the absence of such incentives") ✓; some offenders [AHRC 2025, p. 35](pin:AHRC-2025#p35 "may be unduly pressured into accepting it") ✓.

**Neural data and the regulatory map.** Neural data is [AHRC 2025, p. 21](pin:AHRC-2025#p21 "highly sensitive and valuable because it can provide unprecedented insights into human thought processes") ✓, yet [AHRC 2025, p. 22](pin:AHRC-2025#p22 "the Privacy Act does not provide any express protection for neural data") ✓. The report notes Colorado and California [AHRC 2025, p. 25](pin:AHRC-2025#p25 "Colorado signed House Bill 24-1058 to define and protect neural data and California has now amended the California Consumer Privacy Act 2018") ✓ and Chile [AHRC 2025, p. 25](pin:AHRC-2025#p25 "Chile has worked to introduce neural protections into its national legal system via constitutional amendment") ✓. It adopts UNESCO's definition [AHRC 2025, p. 8](pin:AHRC-2025#p8 "the Commission has adopted the definition set out by the United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization (UNESCO)") ✓ and records the Council's commissioned report [AHRC 2025, p. 9](pin:AHRC-2025#p9 "The UN Human Rights Council adopted a resolution to commission a report on neurotechnology and human rights. The report was released in September 2024") ✓, which is A/HRC/57/61.

## How NOT to read this

1. **The "art 19.1" error (p. 33).** [AHRC 2025, p. 33](pin:AHRC-2025#p33 "the right to security of the person in art 19.1 ICCPR which provides that everyone has the right to ’hold opinions without interference’") ✓ conflates article 9(1) (security of person) with article 19(1) (opinion). The Committee's words it then quotes, [AHRC 2025, p. 33](pin:AHRC-2025#p33 "security of the person includes ‘freedom of injury to the body and the mind, or bodily or mental integrity’") ✓, come from its General Comment on article 9 (General Comment No. 35, not in the library; the AHRC's endnote 169, PDF p. 79, cites CCPR/C/GC/35, para. 3). Cite [[iccpr-art-9]] for security of person and [[ccpr-c-gc-34]] for article 19(1). Never repeat the AHRC's article number as correct.
2. **"UN Human Rights Advisory Committee" (p. 33)** is the Human Rights Council Advisory Committee, an expert body of the Council, not a treaty body: [[a-hrc-57-61]], register (c).
3. **The brain-fingerprinting case study (p. 32).** [AHRC 2025, p. 32](pin:AHRC-2025#p32 "with a statistical confidence of 99.9%") ✓ is secondary-sourced (the endnotes cite the technique's inventor) and scientifically contested. Never repeat the figure as fact: [[reliability-and-admissibility]].
4. **Australian law does not travel.** The Privacy Act analysis, the Evidence Act 1977 (Qld), R v Murray, the Lie Detector Act 1983 (NSW) [AHRC 2025, p. 32](pin:AHRC-2025#p32 "The use of polygraph testing is specifically regulated in New South Wales (NSW) through the Lie Detector Act 1983 (NSW)") ✓, VERA-2R, Static-99R and the ALRC machinery are domestic: comparative material, not international law.
5. **Status of chapter 7.** "Initial observations" (p. 31); the moratorium is a "preliminary view" (p. 32); Recommendation 6 is addressed to a government and is not an obligation. The report itself says the topic needs a standalone report.
