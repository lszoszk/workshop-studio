# Prainsack et al. 2022: Data solidarity

**Full cite.** Barbara Prainsack, Seliem El-Sayed, Nikolaus Forgó, Łukasz Szoszkiewicz and Philipp Baumer, "Data solidarity: a blueprint for governing health futures", *The Lancet Digital Health*, vol. 4 (November 2022), pp. e773–e774, [doi:10.1016/S2589-7500(22)00189-3](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2589-7500(22)00189-3), CC BY-NC-ND 4.0. A two-page Comment; the authors are at the University of Vienna and, for Szoszkiewicz, Adam Mickiewicz University in Poznań (author block on PDF p. 2, not in the passages).

**Register.** (f) scholarship. Łukasz Szoszkiewicz, the trainer of this workshop, is a co-author. It is a position piece in a medical journal, not a legal analysis: no treaty provision is cited. Weight ½ on the ladder.

**One-liner.** Giving individuals more control over their data is not enough; "data solidarity" would govern data by the public value and risk of each use, through three pillars: facilitate good uses, prevent and mitigate harm, return profits to the public.

## Key takeaways

**The problem.** Digital technologies increase inequalities, and people harmed by data use often have no remedy, "either because they cannot prove who and what caused the harm, or because no law was broken" [Prainsack et al. 2022, p. e773](pin:Prainsack-et-al-2022#p1 "Individuals harmed by data use often have no access to remedies, either because they cannot prove who and what caused the harm, or because no law was broken") ✓.

**Control is not enough.** Frameworks built on individual control "are not enough": control "cannot address the vast power asymmetries between data processors and data subjects", and harm "can affect wider groups of people than only the primary data subjects" [Prainsack et al. 2022, p. e773](pin:Prainsack-et-al-2022#p1 "regulatory frameworks that seek to prevent harm mostly by strengthening the control that individuals have over their data are not enough. Although individual control is important, it cannot address the vast power asymmetries between data processors and data subjects … harm—just as benefits— can affect wider groups of people than only the primary data subjects") ✓.

**The public-value test.** Data use "creates public value when it benefits people and communities without posing grave risks"; use with little public value but substantial risk "should be prohibited, with fines severe enough to deter even powerful corporations"; low-risk, high-benefit use "should receive more public support" [Prainsack et al. 2022, p. e773](pin:Prainsack-et-al-2022#p1 "Data use creates public value when it benefits people and communities without posing grave risks … Data use that creates little or no public value but poses substantial risks should be prohibited, with fines severe enough to deter even powerful corporations from breaking the law … should receive more public support") ✓.

**Three pillars.** Data solidarity "complements and helps to realise justice" and "consists of three main pillars" [Prainsack et al. 2022, p. e773](pin:Prainsack-et-al-2022#p1 "It consists of three main pillars: facilitating good data use (pillar 1); preventing and mitigating harm (pillar 2); and returning profits to the public domain (pillar 3)") ✓.

- *Pillar 1.* High-value uses, "often in non-profit health research", are blocked by onerous regulation or practical barriers and should get support "by removing red tape or by providing financial and practical assistance" [Prainsack et al. 2022, p. e773](pin:Prainsack-et-al-2022#p1 "Data solidarity requires that such data uses receive more public support by removing red tape or by providing financial and practical assistance") ✓.
- *Pillar 2.* Grave-risk uses call for "the full force of the law"; for large corporations, multiplied fines for repeat offenders and cross-border enforcement; and, since some risks remain even in beneficial uses, "harm mitigation bodies" to complement legal remedies [Prainsack et al. 2022, p. e773](pin:Prainsack-et-al-2022#p1 "data solidarity requires making use of the full force of the law to prohibit such harmful practices … One way to reach this goal is the creation of harm mitigation bodies at regional, national, or supranational level that would complement existing legal remedies") ✓.
- *Pillar 3.* Where profits accrue, "some of these profits need to come back to the people and communities that enabled them", through digital corporate taxation or data commons governed "by the values of fairness, equality, justice, and sustainability" [Prainsack et al. 2022, p. e774](pin:Prainsack-et-al-2022#p2 "some of these profits need to come back to the people and communities that enabled them in the first place … data commons, in which data and other digital resources are governed collaboratively, guided by the values of fairness, equality, justice, and sustainability") ✓.

**Closing claim.** "Societies have a collective responsibility" to ensure digital practices improve all lives and that harms are prevented; data solidarity "provides a blueprint" [Prainsack et al. 2022, p. e774](pin:Prainsack-et-al-2022#p2 "Societies have a collective responsibility to ensure that digital practices help to improve the lives of all people, and that harms are prevented more effectively. Data solidarity provides a blueprint of how to make this happen") ✓.

## How NOT to read this

- **Not about neurotechnology.** The words neurotechnology, neural data and brain do not appear. The article is about health and digital data in general. Its application to neural data is made by [[szoszkiewicz-yuste-2025]] (p. 3471, "echoing the concept of data solidarity") and by this wiki's [[data-solidarity]] page: an analogy drawn by others, not a claim of this article.
- **Not anti-consent.** "Individual control is important"; the claim is that it is insufficient. Do not cite it as rejecting consent-based protection.
- **Power asymmetry means processors versus data subjects**, mostly companies and citizens. Transferring the point to the State and a detainee ([[consent-in-custody]]) is a further analogy; the documents that make the custodial point are the Secretary-General and UNODC–INTERPOL, not this article.
- **Proposals, not law.** Harm mitigation bodies, multiplied fines, digital taxes and data commons are "one way", "could", "proposed". No instrument in the library adopts them.
- **The figure.** The public-value/risk matrix refers to a figure on PDF p. 1 that the passages do not reproduce; open the PDF to see it.
- **Register.** A Comment in a medical journal; no human rights instrument is cited. It cannot support a claim about what the Covenant requires.
