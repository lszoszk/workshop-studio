# Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025: Mental privacy

**Full cite.** Łukasz Szoszkiewicz and Rafael Yuste, "Mental privacy: navigating risks, rights and regulation", *EMBO Reports*, vol. 26 (July 2025), pp. 3469–3473, published online 25 June 2025, [doi:10.1038/s44319-025-00505-6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s44319-025-00505-6), CC BY 4.0. A five-page "Science & Society" piece. Its disclosure statement (PDF p. 5, not in the passages) says both authors are affiliated with the Neurorights Foundation.

**Register.** (f) scholarship. Łukasz Szoszkiewicz, the first author, is the trainer of this workshop. Read the page as his published position, pinned like any other source, not as authority: it weighs ½ on the ladder, and one General Comment outweighs it.

**One-liner.** Neural data are uniquely sensitive and largely unprotected outside medicine; the article maps the rules as of 2025 (Chile, three US states, the EU, soft law) and proposes three additions: user agency, restricted circulation (data solidarity or a medical-device model) and precaution.

## Key takeaways

**Definitions.** Mental privacy is "defined here as the protection of the mental activity of an individual"; neural data "are uniquely sensitive because they reveal our most intimate processes" and "reflect subconscious and involuntary activity" [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3470](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p2 "Mental privacy, defined here as the protection of the mental activity of an individual … Neural data are uniquely sensitive because they reveal our most intimate processes—thoughts, memories, mental states, emotions, behavior, personality and health conditions—and can even forecast future behavior … these signals also reflect subconscious and involuntary activity") ✓. Neural data are "information generated by measuring activity in the nervous systems, sometimes extending to inferences drawn from that data", "not limited to electrical brainwaves", and include peripheral signals such as EMG [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3470](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p2 "information generated by measuring activity in the nervous systems, sometimes extending to inferences drawn from that data … neural data is not limited to electrical brainwaves … Some peripheral biosignals should also fall under the category of neural data: for example, muscle nerve signals (EMG)") ✓. The regulatory section uses a second, wider formulation [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3471](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p3 "Neural data, defined here as any data collected by neurotechnological devices, are not classified as sensitive information when collected outside medical contexts by consumer products") ✓.

**Capabilities and risks.** Implanted sensors decoded attempted speech in ALS patients at "accuracy levels as high as 97.5%" [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3469](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p1 "successfully decoded attempted speech in individuals with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), reaching accuracy levels as high as 97.5%") ✓; consumer EEG headsets decode attention and basic emotions "although with considerable variability between individuals" [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3470](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p2 "These devices can achieve satisfactory accuracy, although with considerable variability between individuals") ✓. The courtroom is named directly: the admission of wearable data in criminal proceedings "is already provoking intense debate", a reason, for the authors, to strengthen the right against self-incrimination [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3470](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p2 "The question of if and how data extracted from wearables should be admitted in criminal proceedings is already provoking intense debate among legal experts … underlining the urgent need to strengthen protections for the right against self-incrimination in the era of neurotechnology") ✓.

**Human rights framing.** Intrusions on mental privacy "may, in some instances, amount to a violation of the freedom of thought and conscience", and neural data "must be treated as fundamentally distinct" [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3470](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p2 "intrusions on mental privacy may, in some instances, amount to a violation of the freedom of thought and conscience, an absolute right protected under international human rights law. If thought is inviolable, so too should be the data that can be used to infer it … neural data must be treated as fundamentally distinct, with enhanced safeguards") ✓.

**Regulatory approaches compared.** Chile's 2021 constitutional amendment protects "cerebral activity and the information drawn from it", applied in a 2023 Supreme Court ruling ordering a company to delete a consumer's neural data [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3471](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p3 "Chile’s pioneering 2021 constitutional amendment, which protects “cerebral activity and the information drawn from it” … a 2023 unanimous ruling by Chile’s Supreme Court ordering a company to delete a consumer’s neural data") ✓. Three US states fold neural data into existing consumer privacy law [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3471](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p3 "Colorado, California and Montana now explicitly include neurotechnology and neural data in their statutory definitions, and apply to these data existing consumer legislation for personal sensitive data") ✓. The GDPR offers "stronger safeguards for neural data than those in many other jurisdictions", yet "neurotechnology is not explicitly mentioned in the GDPR" [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3471](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p3 "already offer stronger safeguards for neural data than those in many other jurisdictions. Nevertheless, neurotechnology is not explicitly mentioned in the GDPR") ✓. Among soft-law steps, the Advisory Committee's call for General Comments is singled out [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3471](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p3 "the UN Human Rights Council Advisory Committee recommended developing General Comments on the freedom of thought and mental integrity for persons with disabilities. General Comments offer authoritative interpretations of treaty obligations") ✓. The authors see a converging definition [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3471](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p3 "“Neural data” is generally defined as information generated by measuring activity in the central or peripheral nervous systems, sometimes extending to inferences drawn from that data") ✓.

**What it proposes.** These steps "may be insufficient, as three critical vulnerabilities persist" [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3471](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p3 "we think that they may be insufficient, as three critical vulnerabilities persist that should be addressed") ✓. First, agency [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3471](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p3 "users need to exert meaningful control over their own neural data … the consumer has no practical way to inspect, amend or delete the data that have been captured") ✓. Second, circulation: a total ban is the "hard-line solution"; the "more balanced alternative" is data solidarity [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3471](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p3 "the circulation of neural data needs to be restricted, instead of driven by market forces … One hard-line solution would be to ban all sharing or trade of neural data. A more balanced alternative—echoing the concept of data solidarity—would treat neural data as a shared resource that serves the public good (Prainsack et al, 2022)") ✓, or else the medical model [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3472](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p4 "Another practical alternative is to follow the medical model and consider all neurotechnology, including consumer-grade, as medical devices") ✓. Third, precaution, including "temporary moratoria" [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3472](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p4 "lawmakers should be guided by the precautionary principle … temporary moratoria on the most controversial neurotechnologies until longitudinal data accrue … guard against both premature bans and unchecked deployment") ✓. The conclusion warns against "alarmist narratives" [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3472](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p4 "it is equally important to avoid alarmist narratives or overhyping potential harms or regulatory overreach") ✓.

## How NOT to read this

- **Not treaty law.** "May, in some instances, amount to a violation of the freedom of thought" is the authors' view. The Committee has not addressed neurotechnology ([[ccpr-c-21-rev-1-add-4]]); the Secretary-General records both sides of the new-rights debate ([[a-81-351]], para. 16; [[new-rights-vs-existing-rights]]).
- **Two definitions of neural data.** Page 3470 defines it by measurement of nervous-system activity (plus inferences); page 3471 by the device ("any data collected by neurotechnological devices"). The second is wider. Quote the one you mean.
- **Laboratory accuracy is not courtroom reliability.** The 97.5% figure is from implanted sensors in a research setting; the article itself reports "considerable variability between individuals" for consumer devices. See [[reliability-and-admissibility]].
- **Options, not one recommendation.** Data solidarity and the medical-device model are alternatives ("would", "could"); moratoria are "temporary" and on "the most controversial" technologies; the article warns against "premature bans".
- **The precautionary principle** is attributed to international human rights law via a cited source (Donders and Plozza, 2023), not via any treaty text or General Comment in the library.
- **Chile 2023.** The article does not name the parties; Futures Lab does (Girardi v. Emotiv): see [[chile-supreme-court-2023]].
- **Cited by the Secretary-General.** A/81/351 cites this article in footnote 7 (to para. 5, PDF p. 3, among the sources for the "broad consensus" that neural data are highly sensitive) and in footnote 25 (to para. 14, PDF p. 5, on consumer neural data reaching State authorities and courts), both read in the PDF. The pins to those paragraphs live on [[a-81-351]].
