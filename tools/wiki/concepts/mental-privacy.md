# Mental privacy

**One-liner.** "Mental privacy" is the protection of a person's mental activity and of the data that can reveal it. In the library it sits where article 17 (privacy) meets article 18 (thought): the Human Rights Committee links the two articles without using the term; the Secretary-General, the AHRC and the scholarship use it; Chile, as reported, has enacted it.

## What the library says

### (b) The Human Rights Committee

The Committee never says "mental privacy", but it joins the two articles in one sentence: [General Comment No. 22, para. 3](pin:CCPR/C/21/Rev.1/Add.4#p2 "In accordance with articles 18 (2) and 17, no one can be compelled to reveal his thoughts or adherence to a religion or belief") ✓. On data it recalls, under article 17, that [General Comment No. 34, para. 18](pin:CCPR/C/GC/34#p4 "every individual should have the right to ascertain in an intelligible form, whether, and if so, what personal data is stored in automatic data files, and for what purposes") ✓ and, where data are incorrect or unlawfully processed, [General Comment No. 34, para. 18](pin:CCPR/C/GC/34#p4 "the right to have his or her records rectified") ✓. Neither General Comment mentions neurotechnology.

### (c) The Secretary-General and UNODC–INTERPOL

The Secretary-General uses the term descriptively. Neurotechnologies [A/81/351, para. 24](pin:A/81/351#p8 "affect physical and mental privacy by exposing the individual’s private space and integrity") ✓; article 17 applies, and [A/81/351, para. 24](pin:A/81/351#p8 "Neurotechnology introduces new threats to privacy by significantly increasing the level of possible interference") ✓. He extends the Committee's reading of article 17 to neural data: the right [A/81/351, para. 25](pin:A/81/351#p9 "protects personal data, communications and the integrity of private life. This protection would include neural data") ✓, and the Committee's requirements on regulation by law, security, access and rectification [A/81/351, para. 26](pin:A/81/351#p9 "would also apply to neural data and other information retrieved through the use of neurotechnologies") ✓. The verb is "would": the application is his, not the Committee's. He points to the Special Rapporteur on the right to privacy's [A/81/351, para. 27](pin:A/81/351#p9 "foundations and principles for the regulation of neurotechnology and the processing of neural data, including the protection of human dignity and mental privacy") ✓ and to a model law ([A/80/283](https://docs.un.org/en/A/80/283), not in the library).

In justice settings he warns that consumer neural data [A/81/351, para. 14](pin:A/81/351#p5 "may reportedly be accessed by State authorities or introduced as evidence in legal proceedings without recognition of its uniquely sensitive character") ✓, and that [A/81/351, para. 30](pin:A/81/351#p10 "Constant monitoring of the brains of those detained as a form of surveillance could cause mental distress") ✓ under article 10(1). Chile's 2023 ruling was [A/81/351, para. 43](pin:A/81/351#p13 "among the first globally to address neural data under the rights to mental privacy and physical and mental integrity") ✓ (see [[chile-supreme-court-2023]]).

UNODC and INTERPOL: [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 4](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p7 "Brain activity is among the most intimate forms of personal information, and any attempt to monitor, decode or manipulate it … without genuine consent would represent a profound intrusion into human dignity, autonomy, and privacy") ✓; and [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 6](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p9 "Individuals must retain control over their neural data") ✓.

### (d) The Australian Human Rights Commission

The AHRC makes mental privacy the first pillar of free thought: the protection against compelled revelation [AHRC 2025, p. 29](pin:AHRC-2025#p29 "confirms that mental privacy is a foundational element of free thought") ✓, and decoding without safeguards [AHRC 2025, p. 29](pin:AHRC-2025#p29 "threatens both freedom of thought and the right to privacy") ✓. On memory recovery it sees [AHRC 2025, p. 33](pin:AHRC-2025#p33 "a real risk of breaching the right to privacy and freedom of thought as protected by arts 17 and 18 ICCPR") ✓. It lists a "right to mental privacy" among the new rights some propose, and treats that debate as settling the other way ([[new-rights-vs-existing-rights]]). One caution: it writes [AHRC 2025, p. 39](pin:AHRC-2025#p39 "As provided for under arts 18.2 and 17 ICCPR, ‘no one shall be compelled to reveal … thoughts’") ✓; those words are the Committee's (General Comment No. 22, para. 3), not the Covenant's.

### (e) and (f)

The Futures Lab speaks of [Futures Lab 2026, p. 4](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p5 "protections for neural privacy") ✓ in US state law and of tools [Futures Lab 2026, p. 7](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p8 "threatening cognitive privacy") ✓. Szoszkiewicz and Yuste define the term: [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3470](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p2 "Mental privacy, defined here as the protection of the mental activity of an individual, presents distinct challenges beyond those of traditional data protection") ✓, because neural signals [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3470](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p2 "also reflect subconscious and involuntary activity, exposing information that individuals may not consciously recognize") ✓. They tie it to article 18: intrusions [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3470](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p2 "may, in some instances, amount to a violation of the freedom of thought and conscience, an absolute right") ✓, and [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3472](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p4 "mental privacy should enjoy enhanced protection as it is intimately tied to freedom of thought") ✓.

## Where the registers agree and part

All registers place mental privacy on both article 17 and article 18, and the Committee's one sentence (General Comment No. 22, para. 3) is the anchor every later document quotes or paraphrases. They part on status. The Committee has no such right by name. The Secretary-General uses the phrase and extends article 17 to neural data with "would". The AHRC treats it as a pillar of article 18, not a separate right. Szoszkiewicz and Yuste call for "a tailored regulatory framework"; the Futures Lab for new standards. Chile, as the Secretary-General and the scholarship report it, has made it a constitutional right.

## Open questions

- Is an interference with mental privacy to be tested under article 17's "arbitrary or unlawful" standard (limitable) or under article 18's unconditional protection? The Secretary-General sets out both (paras. 20 and 24) without saying which governs decoding in an interrogation.
- Does "mental privacy" cover inferences (emotions, reactions) as well as the raw signal? The scholarship says yes; the Committee's texts speak of "thoughts" and "personal data".
- Whether neural data need a category of their own is argued on [[neural-data]] and [[is-neural-data-special]].
