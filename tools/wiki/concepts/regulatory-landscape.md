# Regulatory landscape

**One-liner.** What law and guidance already exist on neurotechnology, as the library reports them: a few national laws, most of them about neural data in consumer markets; soft-law instruments at the international level; almost nothing written for police, courts and prisons.

## What the library says

No treaty (a) or General Comment (b) in the library deals with neurotechnology. Everything below is reported by (c)–(f) documents, and domestic law is known only second-hand: check the law itself before relying on it.

**The overall picture.** The Secretary-General finds the national response "still nascent and uneven" [A/81/351, para. 42](pin:A/81/351#p13 "At the national level, the regulatory response to the human rights challenges posed by neurotechnology is still nascent and uneven") ✓, and concludes that international human rights law "provides an applicable framework" that needs "further clarification and consistent application" [A/81/351, para. 48](pin:A/81/351#p15 "International human rights law provides an applicable framework to address the risks of neurotechnology, but further clarification and consistent application are needed.") ✓. For criminal justice in particular, UNODC and INTERPOL note "few legal frameworks in place to criminalize the malicious or irresponsible use of neurotechnologies" [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 3](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p6 "An important consideration is that there are currently few legal frameworks in place to criminalize the malicious or irresponsible use of neurotechnologies.") ✓ and that courts assess neuroimaging evidence "on a case-by-case basis" (see [[reliability-and-admissibility]]) [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 4](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p7 "While there is no standardized approach to this globally, courts in some jurisdictions have assessed the admissibility of neuroimaging evidence on a case-by-case basis") ✓.

**International and regional soft law (c).** The Secretary-General lists the Inter-American Declaration of 2023, the UNESCO Recommendation adopted in 2025 ([[unesco-recommendation-2025]]), the OECD recommendation of 2019, a Council mandate for Advisory Committee guidelines, and the UNODC–INTERPOL collaboration [A/81/351, para. 46](pin:A/81/351#p14 "At the international level, the General Conference of UNESCO adopted its Recommendation on the Ethics of Neurotechnology in 2025, which calls for national coordinating bodies for neurotechnology, among other measures.") ✓. The Human Rights Council "has tasked its Advisory Committee with drafting guidelines" for its sixty-fourth session [A/81/351, para. 46](pin:A/81/351#p14 "The Human Rights Council has tasked its Advisory Committee with drafting guidelines on applying existing human rights standards throughout the neurotechnology life cycle") ✓: a follow-up to the Committee's report A/HRC/57/61 ([[a-hrc-57-61]], in the inbox).

**Latin America.** Chile's 2021 constitutional amendment and the Supreme Court's 2023 ruling ([[chile-supreme-court-2023]]); a proposed general law in Mexico; neural data as sensitive data in Brazil and "neurorights" in the constitution of Rio Grande do Sul [A/81/351, para. 43](pin:A/81/351#p13 "In Brazil, neural data is classified as sensitive personal data, and Rio Grande do Sul has enshrined “neurorights” protections in its state constitution.") ✓. The AHRC adds draft amendments to Argentina's Criminal Procedure Code, the one justice-specific national measure in the library [AHRC 2025, p. 25](pin:AHRC-2025#p25 "In Argentina draft amendments to the Criminal Procedure Code addressed neurorights – expressly guaranteeing mental privacy and ensuring the protection of neural data.") ✓.

**Europe.** Under the EU AI Act, neurotechnologies "may be classified as high-risk"; France and Spain have their own texts [A/81/351, para. 44](pin:A/81/351#p13 "under the Artificial Intelligence Act of 2024, artificial intelligence-driven neurotechnologies and emotion-recognition systems may be classified as high-risk where they involve profiling or affect fundamental rights") ✓. Szoszkiewicz & Yuste read the GDPR as already offering "stronger safeguards for neural data than those in many other jurisdictions", while its application is "a subject to debate" [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3471](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p3 "GDPR’s definitions of personal and sensitive data—and its enforcement mechanisms —already offer stronger safeguards for neural data than those in many other jurisdictions.") ✓. Futures Lab holds the EU up as the model for a US federal framework [Futures Lab 2026, p. 8](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p9 "To leverage this, the United States must implement a federal framework grounded in human rights law using the EU model.") ✓.

**United States and China.** No federal law; state laws extend sensitive-data rules to neural data ([[colorado-neural-data-law]], [[california-neural-data-law]]). The documents do not agree on how many states:

- California, Colorado and Montana, says the Secretary-General [A/81/351, para. 45](pin:A/81/351#p14 "While the United States has no federal neurotechnology-specific laws, California, Colorado and Montana have extended sensitive data protections to neural data") ✓, as do Szoszkiewicz & Yuste, who add six states "considering similar legislation" [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3471](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p3 "Colorado, California and Montana now explicitly include neurotechnology and neural data in their statutory definitions") ✓;
- Colorado and California, says the AHRC, without saying "only" [AHRC 2025, p. 25](pin:AHRC-2025#p25 "For example in the US, Colorado signed House Bill 24-1058 to define and protect neural data and California has now amended the California Consumer Privacy Act 2018") ✓;
- Futures Lab says both "currently the only states" [Futures Lab 2026, p. 4](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p5 "California and Colorado are currently the only states to have passed legislation on neural data privacy, while a dozen others have legislation pending.") ✓ and, four pages later, "at least eight" [Futures Lab 2026, p. 8](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p9 "At least eight U.S. states have enacted legislation to protect neurorights") ✓.

China adopted ethical guidelines for brain-computer interface research in 2024 [A/81/351, para. 45](pin:A/81/351#p14 "In 2024, China adopted ethical guidelines for brain-computer interface research covering research integrity, scientific validity and informed consent.") ✓.

## How NOT to read this

- **A snapshot, taken at different times.** Szoszkiewicz & Yuste (mid-2025) still describe UNESCO's text as "a draft instrument … currently under intergovernmental negotiation" [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3471](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p3 "UNESCO published a draft instrument in 2024, currently under intergovernmental negotiation.") ✓; the Secretary-General (August 2026) reports it adopted. Date every regulatory claim.
- **Mostly consumer and data law.** Almost every measure listed governs neural data in markets or research, not the use of neurotechnology by the State against a suspect or prisoner. Do not read a consumer privacy law as a rule of criminal procedure.
- **Second-hand.** The library holds none of these laws. Where documents disagree (the US count above), the wiki records the disagreement rather than choosing.

## Open questions

- Will the Advisory Committee's guidelines (sixty-fourth session) address criminal justice specifically? Ingesting [[a-hrc-57-61]] shows what its first report said.
- Does "neurorights" legislation (Chile, Rio Grande do Sul) protect more in practice than data-protection law (GDPR, the US states)? That is the question of [[new-rights-vs-existing-rights]].
