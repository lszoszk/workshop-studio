# A moratorium on neurotechnology in criminal justice

**One-liner.** Only one document in the library asks for a moratorium: the Australian Human Rights Commission, an NHRI, which would suspend the use of neurotechnology in the criminal justice system until a law reform inquiry has reported. The UN documents take another route: prohibit compulsory or coercive uses outright, and surround any lawful use with validation, consent rules and oversight. The real disagreement is not "moratorium or nothing" but "moratorium or regulated use with safeguards".

## What the library says

### (b) The Human Rights Committee

Neither General Comment mentions a moratorium or neurotechnology. General Comment No. 32 does settle who decides admissibility: within the limits of article 7, "it is primarily for the domestic legislatures of States parties to determine the admissibility of evidence and how their courts assess it" [General Comment No. 32, para. 39](pin:CCPR/C/GC/32#p12 "subject to the limitations on the use of statements, confessions and other evidence obtained in violation of article 7 … it is primarily for the domestic legislatures of States parties to determine the admissibility of evidence and how their courts assess it") ✓. An interim rule of inadmissibility, such as the AHRC proposes, is a domestic choice the Covenant leaves open: neither required nor forbidden.

### (c) The Secretary-General, A/81/351

The word "moratorium" does not appear. The Secretary-General recommends a standing prohibition of a class of uses: Member States should "Prohibit the compulsory or coercive use of neurotechnologies in the administration of justice, including brain-based deception detection, predictive applications, decoding applications" and "neuromodulation" [A/81/351, para. 50 (c)](pin:A/81/351#p15 "Prohibit the compulsory or coercive use of neurotechnologies in the administration of justice, including brain-based deception detection, predictive applications, decoding applications to “read” thoughts, intentions or memories, neuromodulation") ✓. What remains is regulated, not suspended: States should "Accompany any lawful deployment of neurotechnology in the administration of justice with effective oversight, accountability and governance mechanisms" [A/81/351, para. 50 (f)](pin:A/81/351#p15 "Accompany any lawful deployment of neurotechnology in the administration of justice with effective oversight, accountability and governance mechanisms and incorporate continuous monitoring and evaluation") ✓. For the applications still in research (wearable monitoring of offenders, neurostimulation for aggression) there is a timing rule: their human rights issues "should be addressed before the technologies are deployed" [A/81/351, para. 12](pin:A/81/351#p5 "Both of these technologies, if ever considered for use in the administration of justice, would likely raise complex human rights issues that should be addressed before the technologies are deployed") ✓.

### (c) UNODC–INTERPOL 2025

The background paper comes closest to a moratorium in substance, without the word: "Technologies should therefore not be deployed until their accuracy, safety, and feasibility have been rigorously demonstrated through independent testing and peer-reviewed research" [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 5](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p8 "Technologies should therefore not be deployed until their accuracy, safety, and feasibility have been rigorously demonstrated through independent testing and peer-reviewed research") ✓. The trigger for lifting the pause is scientific, paired with a necessity test: "A demonstrable and significant benefit for law enforcement functions must also be shown, and the necessity and proportionality of their use assessed against the potential human rights impacts" [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 3](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p6 "A demonstrable and significant benefit for law enforcement functions must also be shown, and the necessity and proportionality of their use assessed against the potential human rights impacts") ✓.

### (d) AHRC 2025

The criminal justice chapter opens with the moratorium: "Until expertise and time can be put towards examining the opportunities and risks of neurotechnology in the criminal justice system, it will be necessary to introduce a moratorium" [AHRC 2025, p. 31](pin:AHRC-2025#p31 "Until expertise and time can be put towards examining the opportunities and risks of neurotechnology in the criminal justice system, it will be necessary to introduce a moratorium") ✓. Recommendation 6 (so numbered in the box on p. 31 and the list on p. 70) asks the Attorney-General to refer an inquiry to the Australian Law Reform Commission: "Until this work is completed there should be a moratorium on the use of neurotechnology in the criminal justice system" [AHRC 2025, p. 70](pin:AHRC-2025#p70 "Until this work is completed there should be a moratorium on the use of neurotechnology in the criminal justice system") ✓. For investigations, the Commission's "preliminary view is that a moratorium on the use of neurotechnology in criminal investigations should be imposed until the ALRC has published the findings of the recommended inquiry", with information so gained "inadmissible at trial" meanwhile [AHRC 2025, p. 32](pin:AHRC-2025#p32 "The Commission’s preliminary view is that a moratorium on the use of neurotechnology in criminal investigations should be imposed until the ALRC has published the findings of the recommended inquiry. Additionally, until that time, information gained via neurotechnology should be inadmissible at trial") ✓. The reasoning is precautionary: "it is preferable to err on the side of caution" [AHRC 2025, p. 32](pin:AHRC-2025#p32 "it is preferable to err on the side of caution") ✓. The recommendation is addressed to an Australian minister, and the same report warns that "decision-makers must also be conscious where they seek to prohibit or reduce access to the technology of the impact this may have in limiting human rights benefits" [AHRC 2025, p. 20](pin:AHRC-2025#p20 "decision-makers must also be conscious where they seek to prohibit or reduce access to the technology of the impact this may have in limiting human rights benefits") ✓.

### (e) Futures Lab 2026

The submission wants new UN standards and, meanwhile, monitoring rather than suspension: "Until such standards are established, the use of neurotechnology, particularly in legal responses to violations of neurorights, should be subject to careful monitoring" [Futures Lab 2026, p. 7](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p8 "Until such standards are established, the use of neurotechnology, particularly in legal responses to violations of neurorights, should be subject to careful monitoring") ✓. It also asks for the "establishment of redlines" [Futures Lab 2026, p. 3](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p4 "The demand for independent audits and evaluations, establishment of redlines, and binding recommendations to enforcement agencies is critical to prevent regulatory capture") ✓.

### (f) Scholarship

Szoszkiewicz and Yuste derive a precautionary approach from human rights law and list "temporary moratoria on the most controversial neurotechnologies until longitudinal data accrue" among possible steps that "guard against both premature bans and unchecked deployment" [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3472](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p4 "Practical steps might include post-market surveillance of consumer EEG devices, mandatory human rights risk assessments, or temporary moratoria on the most controversial neurotechnologies until longitudinal data accrue … guard against both premature bans and unchecked deployment") ✓. A targeted, time-limited moratorium in a consumer and research context, not a general one on criminal justice.

## Open questions

- Scope. The AHRC's moratorium covers "the use of neurotechnology in the criminal justice system" without exception. Would it reach neuroimaging offered by a defendant in mitigation (see [[reliability-and-admissibility]]), or an assistive brain-computer interface a defendant needs to communicate (the AHRC's own caution on p. 20)? The report does not say.
- The trigger. Four documents name four different events that would end the pause: a domestic inquiry (AHRC), scientific validation (UNODC–INTERPOL), new UN standards (Futures Lab), longitudinal data (Szoszkiewicz & Yuste). They are not the same clock.
- Equivalence. If consent in custody is as doubtful as A/81/351 para. 29 says, a prohibition of "compulsory or coercive" use may suspend most uses in practice: see [[consent-in-custody]]. The Advisory Committee's report [[a-hrc-57-61]] is in the inbox; read its recommendations against this card once ingested.
