# Consent in custody

**One-liner.** Every document that addresses consent to neurotechnology in detention, interrogation or sentencing treats it as fragile. The Secretary-General would require free, prior, informed, explicit and revocable consent and subject it to "the highest evidentiary standards"; UNODC and INTERPOL want consent "free of coercion or undue influence, particularly in custodial or correctional settings"; the AHRC doubts that consent given against a reduced sentence is voluntary. No document says consent alone makes brain-based lie detection lawful.

Terms this page covers: consent, voluntary and voluntariness, free and informed consent, genuine consent, inducement (a reduced sentence, immunity, early release, parole), coercion, undue influence, power imbalance, custody, detention, correctional settings, "offered".

## What the library says

### (a) Treaty text

Article 7 of the Covenant adds, after the prohibition of torture, that no one shall be subjected without his free consent to medical or scientific experimentation ([ICCPR art. 7](https://www.ohchr.org/en/instruments-mechanisms/instruments/international-covenant-civil-and-political-rights), not in the library); see [[iccpr-art-7]]. The Secretary-General and the AHRC both quote that sentence (below).

### (b) The Human Rights Committee, General Comment No. 32

The Committee's words concern confessions, not consent to a test. The article 14(3)(g) standard already reaches indirect and psychological pressure: "the absence of any direct or indirect physical or undue psychological pressure from the investigating authorities on the accused" [General Comment No. 32, para. 41](pin:CCPR/C/GC/32#p13 "the absence of any direct or indirect physical or undue psychological pressure from the investigating authorities on the accused, with a view to obtaining a confession of guilt") ✓. Where article 7 is in issue, "the burden is on the State to prove that statements made by the accused have been given of their own free will" [General Comment No. 32, para. 41](pin:CCPR/C/GC/32#p13 "the burden is on the State to prove that statements made by the accused have been given of their own free will") ✓. Exculpatory material the defence must see includes "indications that a confession was not voluntary" [General Comment No. 32, para. 33](pin:CCPR/C/GC/32#p10 "indications that a confession was not voluntary") ✓. The General Comment does not say what makes consent to a procedure free; the reports supply that. See [[general-comment-32]].

### (c) The Secretary-General (A/81/351) and UNODC–INTERPOL 2025

Consent is the first of four concerns the Secretary-General attaches to deception detection, "consent, coercion, reliability, and the privilege against self-incrimination" [A/81/351, para. 9](pin:A/81/351#p4 "raise serious concerns regarding consent, coercion, reliability, and the privilege against self-incrimination") ✓, and he records that "voluntary use appears to have continued in practice" [A/81/351, para. 9](pin:A/81/351#p4 "voluntary use appears to have continued in practice") ✓ after the Indian court struck down involuntary administration (see [[brain-based-lie-detection]]).

The fullest treatment is in the article 7 section, on neuromodulation of offenders. The report attributes to the Human Rights Council Advisory Committee the view that such techniques "may constitute treatment contrary to articles 7 or 10 of the Covenant when imposed without genuinely free and informed consent" [A/81/351, para. 29](pin:A/81/351#p10 "may constitute treatment contrary to articles 7 or 10 of the Covenant when imposed without genuinely free and informed consent") ✓ (the Advisory Committee's own report, [[a-hrc-57-61]], is in the inbox and is not pinned here). It then states in its own voice that "In custodial settings, the pronounced power imbalance between the State and the individual may call into question the genuineness of consent", and attributes to experts the view that where techniques "are presented in connection with prospects such as early release or favourable parole outcomes, there is a significant risk that consent may not be fully free or voluntary" [A/81/351, para. 29](pin:A/81/351#p10 "In custodial settings, the pronounced power imbalance between the State and the individual may call into question the genuineness of consent. Experts affirm that where such techniques are presented in connection with prospects such as early release or favourable parole outcomes, there is a significant risk that consent may not be fully free or voluntary") ✓ (its footnote 56, on the same PDF page, cites a UNODC submission and the AHRC report). It quotes article 7's free-consent sentence and adds that "The difficulty of establishing that consent is genuinely free given the particularly vulnerable status of those who are detained has been acknowledged by the Human Rights Committee" [A/81/351, para. 29](pin:A/81/351#p10 "The difficulty of establishing that consent is genuinely free given the particularly vulnerable status of those who are detained has been acknowledged by the Human Rights Committee") ✓. That acknowledgement is reported, not pinned: footnotes 57 and 58 on the same PDF page cite General Comment No. 20 (1992), para. 7, which is not in the library. See [[neuromodulation-of-offenders]].

The recommendations set consent as a gate, never as a licence. States should "Prohibit the compulsory or coercive use of neurotechnologies in the administration of justice" [A/81/351, para. 50 (c)](pin:A/81/351#p15 "Prohibit the compulsory or coercive use of neurotechnologies in the administration of justice, including brain-based deception detection") ✓; "Require free, prior, informed, explicit and revocable consent for any potential use of neurotechnology in the administration of justice, accompanied by full transparency regarding risks, access to independent legal counsel of one’s own choice and effective avenues for redress", and, "Given the constrained and coercive nature of justice settings, any consent should be subject to the highest evidentiary standards and rigorous scrutiny" [A/81/351, para. 50 (e)](pin:A/81/351#p15 "Require free, prior, informed, explicit and revocable consent for any potential use of neurotechnology in the administration of justice, accompanied by full transparency regarding risks, access to independent legal counsel of one’s own choice and effective avenues for redress. Given the constrained and coercive nature of justice settings, any consent should be subject to the highest evidentiary standards and rigorous scrutiny") ✓; and "Ensure enhanced safeguards for children, detainees" and others "with particular attention to the conditions of consent" [A/81/351, para. 50 (g)](pin:A/81/351#p16 "Ensure enhanced safeguards for children, detainees … with particular attention to the conditions of consent") ✓. Read together, 50 (c) and 50 (e) leave room for a consented use that passes "the highest evidentiary standards"; the report never says which use would.

UNODC and INTERPOL make the same two moves. Without consent the intrusion is grave: "any attempt to monitor, decode or manipulate it for identification, investigative, preventive or therapeutic purposes without genuine consent would represent a profound intrusion into human dignity, autonomy, and privacy" [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 4](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p7 "any attempt to monitor, decode or manipulate it for identification, investigative, preventive or therapeutic purposes without genuine consent would represent a profound intrusion into human dignity, autonomy, and privacy") ✓. And consent can itself be a form of coercion: "there is concern about the potential for neurotechnology to be used coercively, or under conditions in which individuals are “offered” the use of these technologies in ways that are coercive, or under the guise of moral-enhancement, or similar" [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 4](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p7 "there is concern about the potential for neurotechnology to be used coercively, or under conditions in which individuals are “offered” the use of these technologies in ways that are coercive, or under the guise of moral-enhancement, or similar") ✓. Among its safeguards: "ensuring voluntary and informed consent from individuals" [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 6](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p9 "key precautionary measures must be implemented, including, but not limited to, establishing clear legal and ethical frameworks to protect human rights, ensuring voluntary and informed consent from individuals") ✓, and "Consent must be voluntary and informed, free of coercion or undue influence, particularly in custodial or correctional settings where power imbalances are acute" [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 6](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p9 "Consent must be voluntary and informed, free of coercion or undue influence, particularly in custodial or correctional settings where power imbalances are acute") ✓. The paper also asks, without answering, whether "courts [can] legally order a criminal defendant, even in narrow circumstances, to cooperate" [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 5](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p8 "Can courts legally order a criminal defendant, even in narrow circumstances, to cooperate with law enforcement in the use of neurotechnologies?") ✓.

### (d) The Australian Human Rights Commission (2025)

The AHRC supplies the concrete scenario. "The right to remain silent not only protects against direct pressure but also indirect pressure, which may arise in informing an accused of the consequences of refusing to undergo questioning via neurotechnology" [AHRC 2025, p. 32](pin:AHRC-2025#p32 "The right to remain silent not only protects against direct pressure but also indirect pressure, which may arise in informing an accused of the consequences of refusing to undergo questioning via neurotechnology") ✓. "if an accused was offered the opportunity for a reduced sentence or some level of immunity in exchange for consenting to the use of neurotechnology during questioning by police, it would be difficult to argue that any consent given was entirely voluntary" [AHRC 2025, p. 32](pin:AHRC-2025#p32 "if an accused was offered the opportunity for a reduced sentence or some level of immunity in exchange for consenting to the use of neurotechnology during questioning by police, it would be difficult to argue that any consent given was entirely voluntary") ✓. The reason is structural: "the imbalance of power in a criminal investigation and the indirect pressure that might attach by virtue of the consequences of refusing to consent" [AHRC 2025, p. 32](pin:AHRC-2025#p32 "Considering the imbalance of power in a criminal investigation and the indirect pressure that might attach by virtue of the consequences of refusing to consent to the use of neurotechnology during questioning, this could foreseeably have serious implications for the right to remain silent and not self-incriminate") ✓. At sentencing the same worry returns: consent "could be used transactionally for sentence reduction or an earlier parole – despite the offender’s preference not to be the subject of a neurointervention in the absence of such incentives" [AHRC 2025, p. 35](pin:AHRC-2025#p35 "consent to the use of neurotechnology could be used transactionally for sentence reduction or an earlier parole – despite the offender’s preference not to be the subject of a neurointervention in the absence of such incentives") ✓; some offenders "may be unduly pressured into accepting it" [AHRC 2025, p. 35](pin:AHRC-2025#p35 "others may be unduly pressured into accepting it") ✓. Its general point, written about medical users rather than detainees, travels in structure if not in finding: "Consent may be illusory when people must make a choice that is starkly binary: either consent to the conditions set, or do not receive the technology" [AHRC 2025, p. 62](pin:AHRC-2025#p62 "Consent may be illusory when people must make a choice that is starkly binary: either consent to the conditions set, or do not receive the technology") ✓. Its remedy for investigations is not a consent test but a moratorium and interim inadmissibility (see [[moratorium]]).

### (e) Civil society

The Futures Lab submission anchors consent in bioethics: UNESCO's Universal Declaration on Bioethics and Human Rights, article 6, requires that "all scientific and medical interventions must be carried out with the individual’s prior, free, and informed consent" [Futures Lab 2026, p. 4](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p5 "all scientific and medical interventions must be carried out with the individual’s prior, free, and informed consent") ✓, and it asks States to "ensure free, prior, and informed consent" [Futures Lab 2026, p. 4](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p5 "ensure free, prior, and informed consent") ✓. It would also cap what consent can do: "Individuals should not be able to consent to overly broad uses of neurotechnology until clear and established neurorights are in place" [Futures Lab 2026, p. 7](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p8 "Individuals should not be able to consent to overly broad uses of neurotechnology until clear and established neurorights are in place") ✓. Its Chilean example is a consumer case, not a custodial one: commercialisation "cannot rely solely on click-through consent" [Futures Lab 2026, p. 2](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p3 "demonstrates that commercialization cannot rely solely on click-through consent") ✓.

### (f) Scholarship

Szoszkiewicz and Yuste write about consumers, not detainees: headsets stream data "under wafer-thin consent" [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3471](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p3 "Consumer-grade headsets often stream raw brain signals or algorithmic inferences under wafer-thin consent") ✓, and "True agency requires that individuals retain ongoing, transparent control over their neural data" [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3471](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p3 "True agency requires that individuals retain ongoing, transparent control over their neural data") ✓. Nothing in the two journal articles addresses consent in custody.

## Open questions

- The library names the pressures (power imbalance, inducements, the consequences of refusal) but gives no test for when consent in custody is free. The Secretary-General's "highest evidentiary standards" is a burden of proof, not a test.
- "Revocable" consent (50 (e)) in a test that runs in minutes: the library does not say what revocation would mean for data already recorded. See [[neural-data]].
- Consent by the accused to an exculpatory test (the AHRC's R v Murray) is a different case from consent sought by the State; no document treats it separately.
- Consent and admissibility are separate questions: a freely consented test can still fail on reliability. See [[reliability-and-admissibility]].
