# Brain-based lie detection

**One-liner.** An umbrella term for techniques that measure brain activity to infer deception, or recognition of crime-related information, in interrogation. The library describes fMRI "lie detection" and the "guilty knowledge" tests sold as "brain fingerprinting" and brain electrical oscillation signature profiling; reports that no method has general acceptance in court; and treats coercive use as a threat to article 14(3)(g), article 7 and freedom of thought. The Secretary-General recommends prohibiting compulsory or coercive use; the AHRC would suspend all use in criminal investigations pending an inquiry.

Terms this page covers: lie detection, deception detection and "brain-based deception technologies" (the Secretary-General's terms), "guilty knowledge" or concealed information testing, brain fingerprinting, brain electrical oscillation signature (BEOS) profiling, fMRI lie detection, polygraph (the predecessor), truthfulness, P300. A search for "lie detection" alone misses A/81/351 paras. 8, 9, 20 and 50 (c), which say "deception detection".

## What the library says

### (b) The General Comments say nothing about lie detection

No General Comment in the library mentions lie detection, deception or neurotechnology. Two Committee standards are applied to it by the reports: the article 14(3)(g) standard, "the absence of any direct or indirect physical or undue psychological pressure from the investigating authorities on the accused" [General Comment No. 32, para. 41](pin:CCPR/C/GC/32#p13 "the absence of any direct or indirect physical or undue psychological pressure from the investigating authorities on the accused") ✓ (see [[self-incrimination]]), and the forum internum rule that "no one can be compelled to reveal his thoughts" [General Comment No. 22, para. 3](pin:CCPR/C/21/Rev.1/Add.4#p2 "no one can be compelled to reveal his thoughts or adherence to a religion or belief") ✓ (see [[freedom-of-thought]]). Both are the Committee's words on other questions; their application to brain-based tests is the reports' work.

### (c) The Secretary-General (A/81/351) and UNODC–INTERPOL 2025

**What it is.** The Secretary-General defines brain-based deception detection as "an umbrella term for technologies that measure central nervous system activity with the goal of “detecting deception”", from which "probabilistic inferences are drawn about certain mental states and about cognitive processes, like memory or recognition of a place" [A/81/351, para. 8](pin:A/81/351#p4 "an umbrella term for technologies that measure central nervous system activity with the goal of “detecting deception”. From the neural data retrieved, probabilistic inferences are drawn about certain mental states and about cognitive processes, like memory or recognition of a place") ✓. He distinguishes fMRI "lie detection" from "“guilty knowledge” or “concealed information” testing, which are commercialized under labels such as “brain fingerprinting” and brain electrical oscillation signature profiling" [A/81/351, para. 8](pin:A/81/351#p4 "“guilty knowledge” or “concealed information” testing, which are commercialized under labels such as “brain fingerprinting” and brain electrical oscillation signature profiling") ✓; the latter "measure physiological or brain responses to determine whether a person recognizes concealed or crime-related information" [A/81/351, para. 8](pin:A/81/351#p4 "measure physiological or brain responses to determine whether a person recognizes concealed or crime-related information") ✓. Such use is rarer than neuroimaging as expert evidence: "Less commonly, neurotechnology has been used in interrogation contexts, especially for brain-based deception detection" [A/81/351, para. 8](pin:A/81/351#p4 "Less commonly, neurotechnology has been used in interrogation contexts, especially for brain-based deception detection") ✓.

**Reliability and courts.** "No brain-based deception detection method has achieved general acceptance for courtroom use" [A/81/351, para. 9](pin:A/81/351#p4 "No brain-based deception detection method has achieved general acceptance for courtroom use") ✓. The report gives two cases. "A United States federal appeals court excluded lie detection evidence based on functional magnetic resonance imaging, finding it insufficiently reliable and not generally accepted under evidentiary standards" [A/81/351, para. 9](pin:A/81/351#p4 "A United States federal appeals court excluded lie detection evidence based on functional magnetic resonance imaging, finding it insufficiently reliable and not generally accepted under evidentiary standards") ✓ (its footnote 16, on the same PDF page, names United States v. Semrau, Sixth Circuit, 12 April 2012). The Supreme Court of India "held that the involuntary administration of brain electrical oscillation signature profiling tests violates the constitutional protection against self-incrimination and renders the resulting evidence inadmissible" [A/81/351, para. 9](pin:A/81/351#p4 "held that the involuntary administration of brain electrical oscillation signature profiling tests violates the constitutional protection against self-incrimination and renders the resulting evidence inadmissible") ✓; para. 32 names the case, and its footnote 63 (PDF p. 11) cites paras. 215–224 of the judgment; see [[selvi-v-state-of-karnataka]]. Yet "voluntary use appears to have continued in practice" [A/81/351, para. 9](pin:A/81/351#p4 "However, voluntary use appears to have continued in practice") ✓ (footnote 18, same PDF page, cites a 2025 news article in Science, not a court record).

**Rights engaged.** Dignity: "If these signals are treated as direct indicators of thoughts, intentions or truthfulness, the use of neural data points could undermine how we understand and value each of us as human beings" [A/81/351, para. 18](pin:A/81/351#p6 "If these signals are treated as direct indicators of thoughts, intentions or truthfulness, the use of neural data points could undermine how we understand and value each of us as human beings") ✓. Freedom of thought: deception detection "could reveal information that the person has not chosen to disclose" [A/81/351, para. 20](pin:A/81/351#p7 "Neurotechnologies used to infer mental states, memory or recognition, through brain-based deception detection technology or decoding, could reveal information that the person has not chosen to disclose") ✓. Articles 7 and 10(1): brain-based lie detection "applied in detention or investigative contexts, may result in mental or physical suffering" [A/81/351, para. 28](pin:A/81/351#p9 "The use of brain-based lie detection, decoding of readable information about thoughts, intentions or memories, coercive interrogation techniques, and neuromodulation, applied in detention or investigative contexts, may result in mental or physical suffering contravening the protection provided under articles 7 and 10 (1)") ✓. Article 14(3)(g): "The coercive use of brain-based lie detection could call into question the privilege against self-incrimination" [A/81/351, para. 32](pin:A/81/351#p10 "The coercive use of brain-based lie detection could call into question the privilege against self-incrimination, protected under article 14 (3) (g) of the Covenant") ✓. Article 9: using "brain-based deception technologies to determine whether a person should be detained, may lead to arbitrary detention" [A/81/351, para. 34](pin:A/81/351#p11 "the use of brain-based deception technologies to determine whether a person should be detained, may lead to arbitrary detention under article 9") ✓. Discrimination: tools rely on "non-representative, male-centric data sets that can distort assessments of veracity" [A/81/351, para. 37](pin:A/81/351#p12 "non-representative, male-centric data sets that can distort assessments of veracity or recidivism risk") ✓. Every verb here is "could" or "may".

**The recommendation.** "Prohibit the compulsory or coercive use of neurotechnologies in the administration of justice, including brain-based deception detection" [A/81/351, para. 50 (c)](pin:A/81/351#p15 "Prohibit the compulsory or coercive use of neurotechnologies in the administration of justice, including brain-based deception detection") ✓. It targets compulsory or coercive use; it does not ask for a ban on voluntary use (see [[consent-in-custody]]).

UNODC and INTERPOL record that neurotechnologies "are increasingly considered for use in criminal investigations for lie detection and risk assessments" [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 1](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p4 "they are increasingly considered for use in criminal investigations for lie detection and risk assessments") ✓, list "memory detection" among potential uses that "vary significantly in their level of scientific validity and technical readiness" [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 3](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p6 "Potential uses include memory detection, risk assessment, the decoding of thoughts in the form of words, or neuroenhancement … these use cases vary significantly in their level of scientific validity and technical readiness") ✓, and state that "questions remain about the accuracy of technologies that seek to detect memory or deception" [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 4](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p7 "questions remain about the accuracy of technologies that seek to detect memory or deception") ✓.

### (d) The Australian Human Rights Commission (2025)

The AHRC places the techniques in a lineage: "Lie detector mechanisms (such as polygraph testing) have been used by authorities since the 1940s. However, there is well established scepticism surrounding their use" [AHRC 2025, p. 31](pin:AHRC-2025#p31 "Lie detector mechanisms (such as polygraph testing) have been used by authorities since the 1940s. However, there is well established scepticism surrounding their use due to their reliance on physiological responses to determine guilt") ✓. It describes the claim as a claim: "operators of neurotechnology claim to be able to detect the presence of concealed information through the monitoring of brain waves", via EEG, fMRI, PET and SPECT, "a method otherwise known as ‘brain fingerprinting’" [AHRC 2025, p. 31](pin:AHRC-2025#p31 "operators of neurotechnology claim to be able to detect the presence of concealed information through the monitoring of brain waves … a method otherwise known as ‘brain fingerprinting’") ✓. It reports, as an allegation, that "police in Dubai are alleged to have already acquired neural devices which have been used in their investigations to determine if a suspect has a guilty mind" [AHRC 2025, p. 31](pin:AHRC-2025#p31 "police in Dubai are alleged to have already acquired neural devices which have been used in their investigations to determine if a suspect has a guilty mind") ✓. When it writes that "The UN has stated that the use of neurotechnology as a form of lie detection is particularly concerning" [AHRC 2025, p. 31](pin:AHRC-2025#p31 "The UN has stated that the use of neurotechnology as a form of lie detection is particularly concerning, and claimed that neurotechnology is already being used for this purpose") ✓, its endnote 143 (PDF p. 78) cites A/HRC/57/61, para. 47: "the UN" here is the Human Rights Council Advisory Committee, whose report waits in the inbox ([[a-hrc-57-61]]), not a treaty body.

**A case study to handle with care.** The AHRC reports that in 1999 Lawrence Farwell administered brain fingerprinting to J. B. Grinder and that the test detected details of the crime "with a statistical confidence of 99.9%" [AHRC 2025, p. 32](pin:AHRC-2025#p32 "After agreeing to do the test, it detected that specific details of the crime were recorded in Grinder’s brain, with a statistical confidence of 99.9%") ✓. The figure is one the AHRC reports from a secondary source: its endnotes 152 and 153 (PDF p. 78) cite a 2012 review by Farwell himself, the developer of the technique. Nothing in the library validates the figure, and the Secretary-General's finding that no method has general acceptance stands against it. Do not repeat it as fact.

The AHRC also notes a different use, stimulation "to reduce the ability of people to lie" [AHRC 2025, p. 32](pin:AHRC-2025#p32 "neurotechnology could be used to reduce the ability of people to lie. For example, transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) or transcranial direct stimulation (TDS) can cause changes in brain activity") ✓, which belongs with [[neuromodulation-of-offenders]].

Its conclusion is procedural. Given "neurotechnology’s ability to overtake a jury’s role in assessing witness credibility and determining truth – in conjunction with grave risks to the right to silence, it is preferable to err on the side of caution" [AHRC 2025, p. 32](pin:AHRC-2025#p32 "given neurotechnology’s ability to overtake a jury’s role in assessing witness credibility and determining truth – in conjunction with grave risks to the right to silence, it is preferable to err on the side of caution") ✓, and so "The Commission’s preliminary view is that a moratorium on the use of neurotechnology in criminal investigations should be imposed until the ALRC has published the findings of the recommended inquiry. Additionally, until that time, information gained via neurotechnology should be inadmissible at trial" [AHRC 2025, p. 32](pin:AHRC-2025#p32 "The Commission’s preliminary view is that a moratorium on the use of neurotechnology in criminal investigations should be imposed until the ALRC has published the findings of the recommended inquiry. Additionally, until that time, information gained via neurotechnology should be inadmissible at trial") ✓. See [[moratorium]]. This is an NHRI's "preliminary view", addressed to one government.

### (e) Civil society

The Futures Lab submission is the one document that calls the prospect promising: "While there is promise in neurotechnology’s assistance in assessing guilt, accountability, and the risk of recidivism in judicial proceedings, it also carries risks regarding access to the regulatory process" [Futures Lab 2026, p. 3](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p4 "While there is promise in neurotechnology’s assistance in assessing guilt, accountability, and the risk of recidivism in judicial proceedings, it also carries risks regarding access to the regulatory process") ✓. It warns that policing use "could expand into brain-reading of suspects or witnesses" [Futures Lab 2026, p. 6](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p7 "its use in policing could expand into brain-reading of suspects or witnesses") ✓, and reports that France "was the first country to allow brain imaging in judicial proceedings with consent … except for functional brain imaging" [Futures Lab 2026, p. 3](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p4 "France was the first country to allow brain imaging in judicial proceedings with consent, for medical or scientific research, or for forensic expertise, except for functional brain imaging") ✓: domestic law, reported by a submission that criticises how it was adopted.

### (f) Scholarship

Neither journal article discusses lie detection. The library's only mention of the P300 is in Szoszkiewicz and Yuste, where consumer EEG headsets "detect brainwaves and “P300” responses to control smartphone applications" [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3470](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p2 "These detect brainwaves and “P300” responses to control smartphone applications, decode states such as attention, relaxation and basic emotions") ✓. The library does not connect the P300 to deception detection, so this wiki does not either.

## Open questions

- Validation: the Secretary-General calls the inferences "probabilistic" and no method generally accepted; the AHRC repeats a 99.9% figure that traces to the technique's developer. The library contains no independent validation study. See [[reliability-and-admissibility]].
- Voluntary use "appears to have continued": the Secretary-General's prohibition targets compulsory or coercive use, and the Indian holding concerns "involuntary administration". Whether consent in custody can be free is the question on [[consent-in-custody]] and [[can-a-suspect-consent-to-lie-detection]].
- Exculpatory use: the AHRC's R v Murray example is a test tendered by the accused; the Secretary-General's framework is written against the State. The library does not say whether an accused may insist on a test.
- Is the output "testimony"? See [[self-incrimination]].
