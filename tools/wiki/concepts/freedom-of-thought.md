# Freedom of thought and the forum internum

**One-liner.** Article 18 of the ICCPR protects thought itself, the forum internum, without any limitation. The library applies that protection to brain decoding and coercive neuromodulation, and adds a four-"pillar" scheme that comes from a Special Rapporteur's report outside the library. The two must be kept apart.

## What the library says

### (b) The Human Rights Committee

The Committee reads article 18(1) as [General Comment No. 22, para. 1](pin:CCPR/C/21/Rev.1/Add.4#p1 "far-reaching and profound; it encompasses freedom of thoughts on all matters") ✓, and the provision [General Comment No. 22, para. 1](pin:CCPR/C/21/Rev.1/Add.4#p1 "cannot be derogated from, even in time of public emergency, as stated in article 4 (2) of the Covenant") ✓.

The forum internum is absolute. Article 18 [General Comment No. 22, para. 3](pin:CCPR/C/21/Rev.1/Add.4#p2 "does not permit any limitations whatsoever on the freedom of thought and conscience … These freedoms are protected unconditionally") ✓, and [General Comment No. 22, para. 3](pin:CCPR/C/21/Rev.1/Add.4#p2 "In accordance with articles 18 (2) and 17, no one can be compelled to reveal his thoughts or adherence to a religion or belief") ✓. Limitations attach only to manifestation ([General Comment No. 22, para. 8](pin:CCPR/C/21/Rev.1/Add.4#p3 "Article 18 (3) permits restrictions on the freedom to manifest religion or belief only if limitations are prescribed by law") ✓). Coercion is read broadly: article 18(2) bars [General Comment No. 22, para. 5](pin:CCPR/C/21/Rev.1/Add.4#p2 "the use of threat of physical force or penal sanctions to compel believers or non-believers … Policies or practices having the same intention or effect") ✓.

Opinion, the neighbour of thought, has the same unconditional cover: article 19(1) is [General Comment No. 34, para. 9](pin:CCPR/C/GC/34#p2 "a right to which the Covenant permits no exception or restriction … No person may be subject to the impairment of any rights under the Covenant on the basis of his or her actual, perceived or supposed opinions") ✓, and [General Comment No. 34, para. 10](pin:CCPR/C/GC/34#p3 "Any form of effort to coerce the holding or not holding of any opinion is prohibited") ✓. None of the General Comments mentions neurotechnology, neural data or "pillars".

### (c) The Secretary-General and UNODC–INTERPOL

The Secretary-General applies the Committee's words to decoding and deception detection, which [A/81/351, para. 20](pin:A/81/351#p7 "could reveal information that the person has not chosen to disclose … Such technologies could thus have a profound impact on the freedom of thought") ✓. He notes the Special Rapporteur on freedom of religion or belief's finding that the right's scope and content [A/81/351, para. 21](pin:A/81/351#p7 "“remain largely underdeveloped and poorly understood” (A/76/380, para. 4)") ✓, and reports that [A/81/351, para. 21](pin:A/81/351#p7 "Experts have argued that four pillars of freedom of thought are most relevant to neurotechnology: the freedom not to disclose thoughts; freedom from punishment for thoughts, real or inferred; freedom from impermissible alteration of thoughts; and an enabling environment for free thought") ✓. In his view [A/81/351, para. 22](pin:A/81/351#p8 "Neurotechnology may potentially affect all four pillars of freedom of thought") ✓, although [A/81/351, para. 22](pin:A/81/351#p8 "neurotechnology is not capable at present of “mind-reading”, it is reportedly capable of inferring emotions and internal reactions based on neural data") ✓. On opinion, [A/81/351, para. 23](pin:A/81/351#p8 "Forced neurological interventions, indoctrination programmes or threats of violence designed to compel individuals to form particular opinions or change their opinion would violate this freedom") ✓.

His recommendations follow: States should [A/81/351, para. 50 (c)](pin:A/81/351#p15 "Prohibit the compulsory or coercive use of neurotechnologies in the administration of justice, including brain-based deception detection, predictive applications, decoding applications to “read” thoughts, intentions or memories, neuromodulation") ✓ and [A/81/351, para. 50 (d)](pin:A/81/351#p15 "Prohibit the use of neural data obtained through torture or ill-treatment, or in violation of the rights to freedom of thought and opinion, as evidence in any proceedings") ✓. UNODC and INTERPOL list freedom of thought among the rights that justice institutions [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 4](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p7 "will need to ensure individuals’ rights to privacy, freedom of thought, bodily integrity, non-discrimination") ✓.

### (d) The Australian Human Rights Commission

The AHRC repeats the Committee's "far reaching and profound" and adds that [AHRC 2025, p. 28](pin:AHRC-2025#p28 "Freedom of thought is considered part of an individual’s forum internum (internal realm) and is non-derogable") ✓, while calling it [AHRC 2025, p. 28](pin:AHRC-2025#p28 "Often described as a ‘forgotten freedom’") ✓. It attributes the pillars to [AHRC 2025, p. 29](pin:AHRC-2025#p29 "The Special Rapporteur on freedom of religion or belief (whose mandate also extends to freedom of thought)") ✓ and reads article 18(2) so that it [AHRC 2025, p. 29](pin:AHRC-2025#p29 "would protect people from being subject to the nonconsensual use of neurotechnology devices that may alter thought or neural patterns or behaviours") ✓. Its remedy is institutional: the Committee should [AHRC 2025, p. 70](pin:AHRC-2025#p70 "provide guidance on freedom of thought and neurotechnologies through the adoption of a general comment on article 18") ✓.

### (e) and (f)

The Futures Lab frames the matter as "neurorights" that [Futures Lab 2026, p. 4](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p5 "align with Articles 12 and 18 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights") ✓ and asks for standards grounded in [Futures Lab 2026, p. 2](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p3 "human dignity, cognitive liberty, and equality") ✓: the Declaration rather than the Covenant, and a term ("cognitive liberty") that no General Comment uses. Szoszkiewicz and Yuste tie the data to the right, [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3470](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p2 "If thought is inviolable, so too should be the data that can be used to infer it") ✓, and report that the Advisory Committee [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3471](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p3 "recommended developing General Comments on the freedom of thought and mental integrity for persons with disabilities") ✓ (see [[a-hrc-57-61]]).

## Two things to keep apart

1. **The absolute protection** is the Committee's (register b): no limitation on thought, no compelled revelation (General Comment No. 22, para. 3). It predates neurotechnology. Its application to decoding is the Secretary-General's and the AHRC's, not the Committee's.
2. **The four pillars** come from the Special Rapporteur's report [A/76/380](https://docs.un.org/en/A/76/380) (not in the library; its symbol is named by A/81/351, para. 21). The Secretary-General attributes them to "Experts", the AHRC to the Special Rapporteur. They are an analytic scheme in registers (c) and (d), not treaty-body interpretation. The second pillar, "punishment for thoughts, real or inferred", goes beyond the words of General Comment No. 22; the nearest Committee text is General Comment No. 34, para. 9, on "actual, perceived or supposed opinions".

Where the registers agree: thought is absolute and non-derogable, and non-disclosure is part of it. Where they part: (b) is silent on neurotechnology; (c) and (d) apply it with "could" and "may"; (d), and the Advisory Committee as reported, ask for a new General Comment; (e) argues from the Declaration and "cognitive liberty".

## Open questions

- Is an inferred emotion a "thought" within General Comment No. 22, para. 3? The Secretary-General separates "mind-reading" from inference without saying where article 18 begins.
- Does "freedom from punishment for inferred thoughts" follow from the Committee's "perceived or supposed opinions" (article 19), or does it need new text under article 18? The library does not say.
- Would the general comment the AHRC asks for adopt the pillars, or the Committee's own two-sentence rule?
