# Neuroprediction

**One-liner.** "Neuroprediction" is the use of brain measurements to estimate what a person will do: the risk of reoffending for sentencing, parole or continued detention, or the risk of offending before any offence. The library treats it as largely experimental, scientifically fragile and legally the most exposed use of neurotechnology in justice: it engages the presumption of innocence, article 9 (liberty), equality before the law and non-discrimination, and the Secretary-General recommends prohibiting its compulsory or coercive use.

## What the library says

### (b) The Human Rights Committee

General Comment No. 32 (2007) is about courtroom practice, not neurotechnology; the application to prediction is the reports' own. The presumption of innocence "guarantees that no guilt can be presumed until the charge has been proved beyond reasonable doubt", and defendants should not be presented "in a manner indicating that they may be dangerous criminals" [General Comment No. 32, para. 30](pin:CCPR/C/GC/32#p9 "guarantees that no guilt can be presumed until the charge has been proved beyond reasonable doubt … Defendants should normally not be shackled or kept in cages during trials or otherwise presented to the court in a manner indicating that they may be dangerous criminals") ✓. Equality of arms demands that each side have "the opportunity to contest all the arguments and evidence adduced by the other party" [General Comment No. 32, para. 13](pin:CCPR/C/GC/32#p3 "demands, inter alia, that each side be given the opportunity to contest all the arguments and evidence adduced by the other party") ✓.

### (c) The Secretary-General, A/81/351

Claim and concern sit in one paragraph: "Some research claims that certain neurotechnologies can assess the risk of recidivism", and "The prospect of identifying individuals as future offenders before an offence has been committed raises particularly serious human rights concerns, including related to the presumption of innocence" [A/81/351, para. 10](pin:A/81/351#p4 "Some research claims that certain neurotechnologies can assess the risk of recidivism … The prospect of identifying individuals as future offenders before an offence has been committed raises particularly serious human rights concerns, including related to the presumption of innocence") ✓. The scientific caveat: "Particular caution is warranted when translating findings established at the group or population level into conclusions about individual persons" [A/81/351, para. 13](pin:A/81/351#p5 "Particular caution is warranted when translating findings established at the group or population level into conclusions about individual persons, given intra- and inter-individual variability and the probabilistic nature of many neuroscientific findings") ✓.

Three rights follow. Fair trial: "predictive neurotechnology purporting to assess future criminality may also undermine the presumption of innocence", and the defence needs "access to the scientific expertise and proprietary data" to challenge it [A/81/351, para. 33](pin:A/81/351#p11 "the use of predictive neurotechnology purporting to assess future criminality may also undermine the presumption of innocence … access to the scientific expertise and proprietary data needed to interpret and challenge neurotechnology-derived evidence") ✓. Liberty: recidivism tools "may lead to arbitrary detention under article 9"; detention "must be based on reasons specific to that individual"; such tools "often rely on statistical correlations from non-representative data sets, potentially leading to prolonged or preventive detention based on what a person might do rather than what the person has done" [A/81/351, para. 34](pin:A/81/351#p11 "may lead to arbitrary detention under article 9 … must be based on reasons specific to that individual … often rely on statistical correlations from non-representative data sets, potentially leading to prolonged or preventive detention based on what a person might do rather than what the person has done") ✓. Legal personality: such use "risks prioritizing biological determinism over recognition of the individual as a legal subject" [A/81/351, para. 35](pin:A/81/351#p11 "Neurotechnology claiming to assess the potential for recidivism risks prioritizing biological determinism over recognition of the individual as a legal subject") ✓. The groups most exposed (women, children, people of African descent) are on [[discrimination]]. The recommendation: prohibit compulsory or coercive use, "including … predictive applications" [A/81/351, para. 50 (c)](pin:A/81/351#p15 "Prohibit the compulsory or coercive use of neurotechnologies in the administration of justice, including brain-based deception detection, predictive applications") ✓.

### (c) UNODC–INTERPOL 2025

The paper lists "risk assessment" among potential uses, which "vary significantly in their level of scientific validity and technical readiness, with many still in experimental or early deployment stages" [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 3](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p6 "Potential uses include memory detection, risk assessment, the decoding of thoughts in the form of words, or neuroenhancement … vary significantly in their level of scientific validity and technical readiness, with many still in experimental or early deployment stages") ✓.

### (d) AHRC 2025

The Commission supplies the definition: an offender's fMRI scan "could be used to determine whether they have biological markers of human behaviours that correlate to behavioural traits – including those that may indicate a heightened risk of reoffending. This is known as ‘neuroprediction’" [AHRC 2025, p. 34](pin:AHRC-2025#p34 "An offender’s brain scan (via fMRI) could be used to determine whether they have biological markers of human behaviours that correlate to behavioural traits – including those that may indicate a heightened risk of reoffending. This is known as ‘neuroprediction’") ✓. Its precedent is algorithmic, not neural: COMPAS is "still used in the US to predict the risk of recidivism, despite recognised findings of racial bias within its algorithm" [AHRC 2025, p. 34](pin:AHRC-2025#p34 "Tools such as the Correctional Offender Management Profiling for Alternative Sanctions (COMPAS) are still used in the US to predict the risk of recidivism, despite recognised findings of racial bias within its algorithm") ✓. Its verdict is double-edged: neuroprediction "is reminiscent of an outdated biological determinism that is an anathema to human rights", yet "it may also provide important insights when making decisions about parole or sentences", so it "should only be used in criminal justice settings after specific research" [AHRC 2025, p. 35](pin:AHRC-2025#p35 "reminiscent of an outdated biological determinism that is an anathema to human rights. However, it may also provide important insights when making decisions about parole or sentences … should only be used in criminal justice settings after specific research") ✓.

### (e) Futures Lab 2026

The submission is the most open to the use: "While there is promise in neurotechnology’s assistance in assessing guilt, accountability, and the risk of recidivism in judicial proceedings, it also carries risks regarding access to the regulatory process" [Futures Lab 2026, p. 3](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p4 "While there is promise in neurotechnology’s assistance in assessing guilt, accountability, and the risk of recidivism in judicial proceedings, it also carries risks regarding access to the regulatory process") ✓.

## Open questions

- Voluntary neuroprediction. A/81/351 para. 50 (c) prohibits "compulsory or coercive" use. The library does not say whether a defendant may offer a predictive scan in their own favour, or whether a parole board may invite one.
- Can research cure the inference problem? The AHRC wants "specific research"; the Secretary-General's warning on group-to-individual inference suggests the gap may be structural, not a matter of more data.
- No document reports an actual sentencing or parole decision based on neuroprediction; the claims are anticipatory. The Advisory Committee's report [[a-hrc-57-61]] is in the inbox.
