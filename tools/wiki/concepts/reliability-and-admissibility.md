# Reliability and admissibility of neuroscientific evidence

**One-liner.** Two questions the library keeps apart: whether neurotechnology-derived evidence is reliable enough to say anything about an individual, and whether a court may admit it. The Human Rights Committee leaves admissibility to domestic law, subject to the exclusion of evidence obtained contrary to article 7. The Secretary-General records courts admitting clinical neuroimaging as corroborative material, finds no deception-detection method generally accepted, and recommends independent validation before admission; UNODC and INTERPOL would deploy nothing until accuracy is demonstrated; the AHRC would make all such information inadmissible in Australia pending an inquiry.

Terms this page covers: reliability, accuracy, validity and scientific validation, admissibility, evidentiary weight, probative value, expert evidence, corroborative versus determinative, the exclusionary rule, equality of arms, contestability, "black box", automation bias. Not in the library by name: Daubert, Frye. United States v. Semrau appears only in a footnote of A/81/351 (footnote 16, PDF p. 4).

## What the library says

### (b) The Human Rights Committee, General Comment No. 32

Admissibility is for domestic law: "subject to the limitations on the use of statements, confessions and other evidence obtained in violation of article 7", "it is primarily for the domestic legislatures of States parties to determine the admissibility of evidence and how their courts assess it" [General Comment No. 32, para. 39](pin:CCPR/C/GC/32#p12 "subject to the limitations on the use of statements, confessions and other evidence obtained in violation of article 7 … it is primarily for the domestic legislatures of States parties to determine the admissibility of evidence and how their courts assess it") ✓. The article 7 limit is absolute: "no statements or confessions or, in principle, other evidence obtained in violation of this provision may be invoked as evidence in any proceedings covered by article 14" [General Comment No. 32, para. 6](pin:CCPR/C/GC/32#p2 "no statements or confessions or, in principle, other evidence obtained in violation of this provision may be invoked as evidence in any proceedings covered by article 14") ✓, and "Domestic law must ensure that statements or confessions obtained in violation of article 7 of the Covenant are excluded from the evidence" [General Comment No. 32, para. 41](pin:CCPR/C/GC/32#p13 "Domestic law must ensure that statements or confessions obtained in violation of article 7 of the Covenant are excluded from the evidence") ✓. Equality of arms "demands, inter alia, that each side be given the opportunity to contest all the arguments and evidence adduced by the other party" [General Comment No. 32, para. 13](pin:CCPR/C/GC/32#p3 "demands, inter alia, that each side be given the opportunity to contest all the arguments and evidence adduced by the other party") ✓, and adequate facilities for the defence "must include access to documents and other evidence" [General Comment No. 32, para. 33](pin:CCPR/C/GC/32#p10 "must include access to documents and other evidence") ✓. The presumption of innocence "imposes on the prosecution the burden of proving the charge, guarantees that no guilt can be presumed until the charge has been proved beyond reasonable doubt" [General Comment No. 32, para. 30](pin:CCPR/C/GC/32#p9 "imposes on the prosecution the burden of proving the charge, guarantees that no guilt can be presumed until the charge has been proved beyond reasonable doubt") ✓. The Committee says nothing about scientific reliability or neurotechnology; the applications below are the reports'. See [[general-comment-32]] and [[ccpr-c-gc-32]].

### (c) The Secretary-General (A/81/351) and UNODC–INTERPOL 2025

**Where evidence is admitted today.** "the most common use of neurotechnology in judicial contexts is the introduction of neuroimaging as medical or expert evidence", and "Where accepted, such material is generally treated as corroborative rather than determinative" [A/81/351, para. 6](pin:A/81/351#p3 "the most common use of neurotechnology in judicial contexts is the introduction of neuroimaging as medical or expert evidence … Where accepted, such material is generally treated as corroborative rather than determinative") ✓. "While the admissibility and evidentiary weight of neuroimaging remain contested, several jurisdictions permit its limited and carefully circumscribed use" [A/81/351, para. 7](pin:A/81/351#p3 "While the admissibility and evidentiary weight of neuroimaging remain contested, several jurisdictions permit its limited and carefully circumscribed use") ✓: courts of appeal in the Netherlands "have accepted neuroimaging and neuropsychological evidence as corroborative material", the Court of Appeal of England and Wales "has accepted neuroscientific evidence as corroborative – using it to support rather than determine assessments of culpability and sentencing", and in Canada it "is not treated as determinative" [A/81/351, para. 7](pin:A/81/351#p3 "have accepted neuroimaging and neuropsychological evidence as corroborative material … has accepted neuroscientific evidence as corroborative – using it to support rather than determine assessments of culpability and sentencing … is not treated as determinative") ✓. These are reports of domestic practice, not a UN endorsement of it.

**Where it is not.** "No brain-based deception detection method has achieved general acceptance for courtroom use" [A/81/351, para. 9](pin:A/81/351#p4 "No brain-based deception detection method has achieved general acceptance for courtroom use") ✓; a United States federal appeals court excluded fMRI lie detection "finding it insufficiently reliable and not generally accepted under evidentiary standards" [A/81/351, para. 9](pin:A/81/351#p4 "finding it insufficiently reliable and not generally accepted under evidentiary standards") ✓ (footnote 16, same PDF page: United States v. Semrau, Sixth Circuit, 2012); the Supreme Court of India held involuntary BEOS tests to violate the privilege and "renders the resulting evidence inadmissible" [A/81/351, para. 9](pin:A/81/351#p4 "renders the resulting evidence inadmissible") ✓ (see [[selvi-v-state-of-karnataka]], [[brain-based-lie-detection]]). For decoding, "Whether such approaches will, at some point, be able to produce information that is sufficiently reliable to be used in the administration of justice is unknown" [A/81/351, para. 11](pin:A/81/351#p4 "Whether such approaches will, at some point, be able to produce information that is sufficiently reliable to be used in the administration of justice is unknown") ✓.

**Why reliability is the key concern.** "A key concern regarding the use of neurotechnologies in the administration of justice is the reliability of neuroscientific evidence in court" [A/81/351, para. 13](pin:A/81/351#p5 "A key concern regarding the use of neurotechnologies in the administration of justice is the reliability of neuroscientific evidence in court") ✓. The report names the inferential step that fails: "Particular caution is warranted when translating findings established at the group or population level into conclusions about individual persons, given intra- and inter-individual variability and the probabilistic nature of many neuroscientific findings" [A/81/351, para. 13](pin:A/81/351#p5 "Particular caution is warranted when translating findings established at the group or population level into conclusions about individual persons, given intra- and inter-individual variability and the probabilistic nature of many neuroscientific findings") ✓, worsened by "the gap between controlled laboratory conditions and real-world settings" and a lack of shared understanding "which makes it difficult for courts to assess the probative value of such evidence" [A/81/351, para. 13](pin:A/81/351#p5 "the gap between controlled laboratory conditions and real-world settings … which makes it difficult for courts to assess the probative value of such evidence") ✓. Its conclusion: "the evidentiary use of neuroscientific data remains contested. Its technical complexity, probabilistic nature and potential for misinterpretation pose significant risks" [A/81/351, para. 47](pin:A/81/351#p14 "the evidentiary use of neuroscientific data remains contested. Its technical complexity, probabilistic nature and potential for misinterpretation pose significant risks") ✓. Consumer data add a second route into court, "introduced as evidence in legal proceedings without recognition of its uniquely sensitive character, a risk amplified in counter-terrorism and national security contexts, where legal thresholds for data access and admissibility are often significantly lower" [A/81/351, para. 14](pin:A/81/351#p5 "introduced as evidence in legal proceedings without recognition of its uniquely sensitive character, a risk amplified in counter-terrorism and national security contexts, where legal thresholds for data access and admissibility are often significantly lower") ✓ (see [[neural-data]]). Two further hazards for the bench: "automation bias – the tendency to accept uncritically the outputs of technological systems" [A/81/351, para. 31](pin:A/81/351#p10 "automation bias – the tendency to accept uncritically the outputs of technological systems") ✓, and systems whose "untransparent and obscure nature" makes them a "black box" [A/81/351, para. 34](pin:A/81/351#p11 "the untransparent and obscure nature of such systems – sometimes referred to as a “black box”") ✓.

**Fair-trial conditions on admission.** Equality of arms "would require the defence to have access to the scientific expertise and proprietary data needed to interpret and challenge neurotechnology-derived evidence" [A/81/351, para. 33](pin:A/81/351#p11 "would require the defence to have access to the scientific expertise and proprietary data needed to interpret and challenge neurotechnology-derived evidence") ✓. "According to submissions and research, the right to a fair trial also depends on requiring that any neurotechnology-based evidence admitted in court meets the highest standards of scientific validation, transparency and contestability" [A/81/351, para. 33](pin:A/81/351#p11 "According to submissions and research, the right to a fair trial also depends on requiring that any neurotechnology-based evidence admitted in court meets the highest standards of scientific validation, transparency and contestability") ✓: attributed to submissions and research, not stated as law. The exclusionary rule "could apply to evidence obtained through the coercive use of neurotechnologies" [A/81/351, para. 32](pin:A/81/351#p10 "This could apply to evidence obtained through the coercive use of neurotechnologies") ✓ (see [[self-incrimination]]).

**Recommendations.** "Require rigorous independent scientific review and validation of any neurotechnology-derived evidence proposed for admission in court, including assessment, as appropriate, of its safety, validity, reliability, reproducibility, known limitations and suitability for the specific context and population in which it is applied" [A/81/351, para. 50 (f)](pin:A/81/351#p15 "Require rigorous independent scientific review and validation of any neurotechnology-derived evidence proposed for admission in court, including assessment, as appropriate, of its safety, validity, reliability, reproducibility, known limitations and suitability for the specific context and population in which it is applied") ✓; "Prohibit the use of neural data obtained through torture or ill-treatment, or in violation of the rights to freedom of thought and opinion, as evidence in any proceedings" [A/81/351, para. 50 (d)](pin:A/81/351#p15 "Prohibit the use of neural data obtained through torture or ill-treatment, or in violation of the rights to freedom of thought and opinion, as evidence in any proceedings") ✓; and "specialized training for law enforcement officials, prosecutors, judges and defence counsel" [A/81/351, para. 50 (j)](pin:A/81/351#p16 "specialized training for law enforcement officials, prosecutors, judges and defence counsel") ✓.

UNODC and INTERPOL note that hospital scans "have for some time been used in expert evidence in some jurisdictions to support claims related to legal insanity, risk, intent, culpability, and type of punishment" [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 1](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p4 "have for some time been used in expert evidence in some jurisdictions to support claims related to legal insanity, risk, intent, culpability, and type of punishment") ✓, and that the new uses raise questions about "how standards of admissible evidence may evolve" and "the reliability of neuroscientific methods, the risk of over-reliance on expert testimony" [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 2](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p5 "how standards of admissible evidence may evolve … the reliability of neuroscientific methods, the risk of over-reliance on expert testimony") ✓. "Many of the technologies under consideration have not been tested for accuracy, reliability, or safety in real-world law enforcement environments" [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 4](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p7 "Many of the technologies under consideration have not been tested for accuracy, reliability, or safety in real-world law enforcement environments") ✓, and "courts in some jurisdictions have assessed the admissibility of neuroimaging evidence on a case-by-case basis" [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 4](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p7 "courts in some jurisdictions have assessed the admissibility of neuroimaging evidence on a case-by-case basis, determining the appropriate conditions under which it can be used") ✓. The paper's rule is validation first: "Technologies should therefore not be deployed until their accuracy, safety, and feasibility have been rigorously demonstrated through independent testing and peer-reviewed research", and courts need "clear and reliable information about what these tools can - and cannot - do" [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 5](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p8 "Technologies should therefore not be deployed until their accuracy, safety, and feasibility have been rigorously demonstrated through independent testing and peer-reviewed research … clear and reliable information about what these tools can - and cannot - do") ✓. It also asks for "clear procedures for maintaining the chain of custody when handling neurotechnology-related evidence" [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 5](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p8 "clear procedures for maintaining the chain of custody when handling neurotechnology-related evidence") ✓ and for "developing clear rules on admissibility of evidence" [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 6](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p9 "developing clear rules on admissibility of evidence") ✓.

### (d) The Australian Human Rights Commission (2025)

The AHRC's material is Australian. In R v Murray, lie detection evidence tendered by the accused "was to bolster the credibility of the accused as a witness, which was a matter for the jury to assess" [AHRC 2025, p. 32](pin:AHRC-2025#p32 "was to bolster the credibility of the accused as a witness, which was a matter for the jury to assess") ✓; the Lie Detector Act 1983 (NSW) "renders lie detection evidence inadmissible in court" [AHRC 2025, p. 32](pin:AHRC-2025#p32 "renders lie detection evidence inadmissible in court and prohibits its use to determine honesty in employment settings") ✓. The Commission concedes possible benefits only "(if the technology is accurate and sufficiently reliable)" [AHRC 2025, p. 32](pin:AHRC-2025#p32 "reduce the number of wrongful convictions via exculpatory evidence (if the technology is accurate and sufficiently reliable)") ✓, and its preliminary view is that, pending an ALRC inquiry, "information gained via neurotechnology should be inadmissible at trial" [AHRC 2025, p. 32](pin:AHRC-2025#p32 "until that time, information gained via neurotechnology should be inadmissible at trial") ✓ (see [[moratorium]]). On risk assessment it doubts accuracy: using neurotechnology "as a factor in determining a person’s chance of reoffending may not be an accurate representation of such a risk" [AHRC 2025, p. 35](pin:AHRC-2025#p35 "using neurotechnology as a factor in determining a person’s chance of reoffending may not be an accurate representation of such a risk") ✓ (see [[neuroprediction]]). It suggests a guide for judges on neurotechnology, modelled on an existing guide on AI [AHRC 2025, p. 34](pin:AHRC-2025#p34 "A similar guide focused on the possible challenges and opportunities that neurotechnology presents for Australian courts and tribunals would be a useful initiative") ✓.

### (e) Civil society

The Futures Lab submission reports accuracy claims from research, not from courts: a BCI used "to translate internal speech with 79 percent accuracy" [Futures Lab 2026, p. 2](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p3 "to translate internal speech with 79 percent accuracy onto a monitor") ✓. It reports that France admitted brain imaging in judicial proceedings, except functional imaging, by a policy "adopted prior to consensus among neuroscience experts" [Futures Lab 2026, p. 3](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p4 "This policy was adopted prior to consensus among neuroscience experts and in opposition to the recommendations of the French Parliamentary Office for the Evaluation of Scientific and Technological Choices") ✓. Its reliability point is about bias: non-diverse training data make "outputs less accurate" [Futures Lab 2026, p. 6](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p7 "making outputs less accurate and potentially harmful to people of African descent and other marginalized communities") ✓ and may yield "inaccurate or pathologizing interpretations of their mental states" [Futures Lab 2026, p. 7](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p8 "neurotechnologies may produce inaccurate or pathologizing interpretations of their mental states") ✓.

### (f) Scholarship

The library's highest accuracy figures come from clinical research with cooperating participants: speech decoding in persons with ALS "reaching accuracy levels as high as 97.5%" [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3469](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p1 "successfully decoded attempted speech in individuals with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), reaching accuracy levels as high as 97.5%") ✓, while consumer devices "can achieve satisfactory accuracy, although with considerable variability between individuals" [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3470](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p2 "These devices can achieve satisfactory accuracy, although with considerable variability between individuals") ✓. Those figures describe attempted speech by volunteers, not deception by suspects; the Secretary-General's warning about the laboratory-to-courtroom gap (para. 13) is the reason they do not carry over. The authors also note that whether wearable data "should be admitted in criminal proceedings is already provoking intense debate among legal experts" [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3470](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p2 "should be admitted in criminal proceedings is already provoking intense debate among legal experts") ✓, and warn against "alarmist narratives or overhyping potential harms" [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3472](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p4 "it is equally important to avoid alarmist narratives or overhyping potential harms or regulatory overreach") ✓.

## Open questions

- Which standard? The Secretary-General speaks of "general acceptance" and "evidentiary standards" without naming Daubert or Frye; the library contains no admissibility test of its own. A State asked to "require rigorous independent scientific review" has no model text in the library.
- Who pays for the defence's expertise? Para. 33 says equality of arms "would require" access to expertise and proprietary data; the library does not say how, or who bears the cost.
- Exculpatory evidence: the AHRC's interim inadmissibility would also exclude tests the accused wants to tender (R v Murray); the Secretary-General's framework is written against the prosecution. Neither addresses the asymmetry.
- "Corroborative rather than determinative" describes practice; no document says what weight is permissible, or how a court keeps corroborative evidence from becoming decisive in effect.
- Consent and reliability are independent: a freely consented test is still probabilistic. See [[consent-in-custody]].
