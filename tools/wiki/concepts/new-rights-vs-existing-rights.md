# New rights or existing rights?

**One-liner.** Should neurotechnology be met with new rights ("neurorights", cognitive liberty, a right to mental privacy) or with the rights already in the treaties, interpreted for it? The UN reports and the NHRI in the library lean to existing rights, clarified; the civil-society submission asks for new UN standards; the article sits in between.

## What the library says

**The question, as the documents put it.** The AHRC states it plainly: create new rights "focused directly on protecting the human mind", or "interpret and adapt existing rights" [AHRC 2025, p. 30](pin:AHRC-2025#p30 "whether new human rights focused directly on protecting the human mind (e.g. right to mental privacy, cognitive liberty etc) should be created, or if it is better to interpret and adapt existing rights in response to new technology") ✓. The case for new rights rests on the claim "that existing rights are insufficient" [AHRC 2025, p. 30](pin:AHRC-2025#p30 "The call for new rights – often called neurorights – is predicated on the basis that existing rights are insufficient to protect against the misuse of neurotechnology.") ✓.

**(b) What existing law already holds.** No General Comment in the library speaks of neurotechnology. What the Human Rights Committee did say, in 1993, is that article 18 "does not permit any limitations whatsoever on the freedom of thought" [General Comment No. 22, para. 3](pin:CCPR/C/21/Rev.1/Add.4#p2 "It does not permit any limitations whatsoever on the freedom of thought and conscience or on the freedom to have or adopt a religion or belief of one’s choice.") ✓. Whether that protection reaches a brain scan is a question of interpretation, which is the whole debate (see [[freedom-of-thought]]).

**(c) The Secretary-General.** The report records both views without choosing between them [A/81/351, para. 16](pin:A/81/351#p6 "While some contend that the challenges of neurotechnology may require development of new legal frameworks, others affirm that the risks of misuse or abuse of neurotechnology may be addressed within the existing international human rights law framework") ✓, then concludes that international human rights law "provides an applicable framework" which needs "further clarification and consistent application" [A/81/351, para. 48](pin:A/81/351#p15 "International human rights law provides an applicable framework to address the risks of neurotechnology, but further clarification and consistent application are needed.") ✓. The Human Rights Council has chosen the same route: guidelines from its Advisory Committee "on applying existing human rights standards" [A/81/351, para. 46](pin:A/81/351#p14 "The Human Rights Council has tasked its Advisory Committee with drafting guidelines on applying existing human rights standards throughout the neurotechnology life cycle") ✓. UNODC and INTERPOL quote the Secretary-General's *Our Common Agenda* on "updating or clarifying" the application of existing frameworks [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 2](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p5 "consideration should, for instance, be given to updating or clarifying our application of human rights frameworks and standards to address frontier issues") ✓.

**(d) The AHRC.** It reads the international debate as "settling in favour of interpreting and adapting existing rights", and leaves neurorights outside its scope [AHRC 2025, p. 30](pin:AHRC-2025#p30 "At the international and transnational level, these debates appear to be settling in favour of interpreting and adapting existing rights.") ✓. Its own answer is a new interpretation, not a new right: a Human Rights Committee general comment on article 18 and neurotechnologies [AHRC 2025, p. 70](pin:AHRC-2025#p70 "The United Nations Human Rights Committee provide guidance on freedom of thought and neurotechnologies through the adoption of a general comment on article 18") ✓.

**(e) Futures Lab.** The submission asks the United Nations to "develop new human rights standards" [Futures Lab 2026, p. 7](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p8 "the United Nations needs to develop new human rights standards that reflect the emerging and rapidly evolving nature of neurotechnology") ✓, while also presenting neurorights as aligned with articles 12 and 18 of the Universal Declaration [Futures Lab 2026, p. 4](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p5 "They align with Articles 12 and 18 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR) which protect privacy and freedom of thought, conscience, and religion.") ✓.

**(f) Szoszkiewicz & Yuste.** They propose "strengthening existing data protection regulations" [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3469](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p1 "We propose strengthening existing data protection regulations with a broad approach to safeguard neural data") ✓, yet also see "growing consensus that additional rules are needed" [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3472](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p4 "national and international regulations signal growing consensus that additional rules are needed") ✓: existing rights, with new, specific rules on top.

## Contested claims

```contested
claim: Existing human rights law, interpreted and clarified, is enough to address neurotechnology in the administration of justice; no new rights are needed.
verdict: leans-support
note: No treaty text or General Comment decides it. The UN reports and the NHRI treat existing law as applicable but in need of clarification; only civil society asks for new standards. "Clarification" can itself mean new instruments (guidelines, a general comment), so the two camps are closer than the labels suggest.
~ c | [A/81/351, para. 16](pin:A/81/351#p6 "While some contend that the challenges of neurotechnology may require development of new legal frameworks, others affirm that the risks of misuse or abuse of neurotechnology may be addressed within the existing international human rights law framework") ✓ | records both views without choosing | [[a-81-351]]
+ c | [A/81/351, para. 48](pin:A/81/351#p15 "International human rights law provides an applicable framework to address the risks of neurotechnology, but further clarification and consistent application are needed.") ✓ | the report's own conclusion: applicable, needs clarification | [[a-81-351]]
+ c | [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 2](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p5 "updating or clarifying our application of human rights frameworks and standards") ✓ | clarify existing frameworks | [[unodc-interpol-2025]]
+ d | [AHRC 2025, p. 30](pin:AHRC-2025#p30 "these debates appear to be settling in favour of interpreting and adapting existing rights") ✓ | an NHRI reads the debate as settling; neurorights left out of scope | [[ahrc-2025]]
- e | [Futures Lab 2026, p. 7](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p8 "the United Nations needs to develop new human rights standards that reflect the emerging and rapidly evolving nature of neurotechnology") ✓ | calls for new UN standards | [[futures-lab-2026]]
~ f | [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3472](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p4 "national and international regulations signal growing consensus that additional rules are needed") ✓ | strengthen existing law, add specific rules | [[szoszkiewicz-yuste-2025]]
```

**Reading.** The weight lies with "existing rights, clarified", but it is the weight of (c) and (d) documents, not of a treaty body. The decisive voice would be the Human Rights Committee itself, which the AHRC asks to adopt a general comment. Until then, this is a policy choice, and an ingest of A/HRC/57/61 ([[a-hrc-57-61]]) adds the Advisory Committee's own view.

## How NOT to read this

- "Appear to be settling" is the AHRC's observation of a debate, not a holding that existing rights suffice.
- Futures Lab's "new standards" need not mean new treaty rights: it frames neurorights through the Universal Declaration.
- Chile's constitutional "neurorights" are domestic law ([[regulatory-landscape]]); they show one country's choice, not the state of international law.

## Open questions

- Is a general comment that reads article 18 to cover neural decoding "interpretation" or a new right in all but name?
- What would a new right protect that article 17 privacy and the absolute forum internum do not?
