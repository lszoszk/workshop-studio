# Neural data

**One-liner.** Data about the structure, activity and function of the nervous system, gathered through neurotechnology. Every register in the library calls them highly sensitive. The documents part on the vehicle of protection: the top tier of existing data-protection law (the Secretary-General, the AHRC, the US statutes as reported) or a category and standards of their own (the Futures Lab and, with qualifications, the scholarship).

## What the library says

### Definitions

The Secretary-General adopts UNESCO's definition: [A/81/351, para. 5](pin:A/81/351#p3 "“qualitative and quantitative data about the structure, activity and function of the nervous system gathered through neurotechnology”") ✓ (its footnote 6, on the same PDF page, cites the UNESCO Recommendation; see [[unesco-recommendation-2025]]). UNODC and INTERPOL: [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 1](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p4 "information obtained by measuring the activity of a person's central or peripheral nervous system") ✓. The scholarship widens it: [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3470](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p2 "neural data is not limited to electrical brainwaves. It encompasses any measurable signal that reflects neural activity, whether obtained electrically, chemically or via other means") ✓, including muscle nerve signals. The Futures Lab reports California's narrower statutory wording: [Futures Lab 2026, p. 4](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p5 "information that is generated by measuring the activity of a consumer’s central or peripheral nervous system, and that is not inferred from nonneural information") ✓. Whether inferences count is therefore unsettled across the library.

### Why they are treated as sensitive

(c) [A/81/351, para. 5](pin:A/81/351#p3 "There is broad consensus that neural data are highly sensitive, providing unprecedented access to internal mental states, and that they require heightened ethical and legal protection") ✓ (its footnote 7, on the same PDF page, cites A/HRC/58/58, Szoszkiewicz and Yuste 2025, and Genser, Damianos and Yuste 2024). Neural data are [A/81/351, para. 25](pin:A/81/351#p9 "uniquely revealing since they are capable of disclosing certain aspects of identity and personality") ✓ and [A/81/351, para. 25](pin:A/81/351#p9 "biometric in character and may reportedly be re-identified across data sets and yield insights that were not foreseeable at the time of collection") ✓. The same report is the library's counterweight on reliability: [A/81/351, para. 13](pin:A/81/351#p5 "Brain data are complex and uncertain, and links between brain activity and behaviour can be misunderstood") ✓. Sensitive is not the same as probative (see [[reliability-and-admissibility]]).

(d) The AHRC: neural data [AHRC 2025, p. 21](pin:AHRC-2025#p21 "is highly sensitive and valuable because it can provide unprecedented insights into human thought processes and functions") ✓; brain scans [AHRC 2025, p. 44](pin:AHRC-2025#p44 "can be as unique as an individual’s fingerprint") ✓. (f) Szoszkiewicz and Yuste: they [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3470](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p2 "reveal our most intimate processes—thoughts, memories, mental states, emotions, behavior, personality and health conditions—and can even forecast future behavior") ✓.

### How the law reaches them

(b) The Committee's article 17 data rights predate neurotechnology: [General Comment No. 34, para. 18](pin:CCPR/C/GC/34#p4 "every individual should have the right to ascertain in an intelligible form, whether, and if so, what personal data is stored in automatic data files, and for what purposes") ✓.

(c) The Secretary-General applies them: article 17's protection of personal data [A/81/351, para. 25](pin:A/81/351#p9 "would include neural data") ✓, and the Committee's requirements [A/81/351, para. 26](pin:A/81/351#p9 "would also apply to neural data and other information retrieved through the use of neurotechnologies") ✓. He concludes that [A/81/351, para. 49](pin:A/81/351#p15 "Neural data, as uniquely sensitive personal data, demand heightened protection, especially considering their increasing collection by private actors and potential reuse in legal proceedings") ✓ and recommends that States [A/81/351, para. 50 (b)](pin:A/81/351#p15 "Recognize neural data as highly sensitive personal data subject to the highest level of legal protection; impose clear limitations on its collection, processing, sale, storage, retention, reuse and sharing") ✓. He reports the same route in domestic law: [A/81/351, para. 45](pin:A/81/351#p14 "California, Colorado and Montana have extended sensitive data protections to neural data") ✓, and in Brazil [A/81/351, para. 43](pin:A/81/351#p13 "neural data is classified as sensitive personal data") ✓. The split he does draw is between devices: invasive ones are licensed medical devices whose data fall under health-data law, while consumer devices [A/81/351, para. 4](pin:A/81/351#p2 "are generally subject to little or no regulation, even though wearable devices are also medicalgrade, and the data that they collect are extremely sensitive") ✓.

(d) The AHRC finds that Australia's Privacy Act [AHRC 2025, p. 22](pin:AHRC-2025#p22 "does not provide any express protection for neural data") ✓ but, being technology-neutral, [AHRC 2025, p. 22](pin:AHRC-2025#p22 "should be adaptable to changing technologies and environments, including neurotechnology") ✓; whether neural data are "personal" or "sensitive" information [AHRC 2025, p. 25](pin:AHRC-2025#p25 "needs to be clarified") ✓.

(e) The Futures Lab accepts that the GDPR [Futures Lab 2026, p. 4](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p5 "ensures that personal and sensitive neurobiometric data about health and mental status are legally classified and protected") ✓, yet asks for more: [Futures Lab 2026, p. 4](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p5 "specific international standards must be established to recognize the highly sensitive and personal nature of bio- and neurotechnologies") ✓.

(f) Szoszkiewicz and Yuste name the gap: neural data [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3471](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p3 "are not classified as sensitive information when collected outside medical contexts by consumer products") ✓. Their answer is [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3469](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p1 "strengthening existing data protection regulations with a broad approach to safeguard neural data") ✓, while also holding that [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3470](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p2 "neural data must be treated as fundamentally distinct, with enhanced safeguards") ✓. A second route they float is the medical model, which [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3472](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p4 "would render all neural data medical and thus, protected by appropriate regulation already implemented in most countries") ✓.

Where the registers agree: sensitivity, and the need for heightened protection. Where they part: the Secretary-General and the AHRC work inside existing categories; the Futures Lab wants specific international standards; Szoszkiewicz and Yuste want both ("fundamentally distinct", by strengthening existing law).

## Open questions

- Do inferences drawn from neural data count as neural data? California's statute, as reported, says no; the scholarship and the Secretary-General's definition leave it open.
- In justice settings, who controls neural data gathered by a consumer device and later sought by a prosecutor? The Secretary-General flags the risk (para. 14) without a rule; [[data-solidarity]] holds the one collective model in the library.
- Does "highest level of legal protection" (para. 50 (b)) mean a consent-only regime, or something stricter in custody? The library does not say.
