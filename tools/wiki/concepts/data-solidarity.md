# Data solidarity

**One-liner.** A way of governing data collectively rather than only through each person's consent: support uses with public value, prohibit harmful ones, return profits to the public. Proposed for health data in 2022; applied to neural data by one article in the library; no UN document, NHRI or civil-society submission in the library uses the term.

## What the library says

Only two documents use the term, both scholarship (f), and both co-authored by the trainer of this workshop. No General Comment (b), UN report (c), NHRI report (d) or civil-society submission (e) in the library mentions data solidarity.

**The idea (health data).** Prainsack et al. start from a diagnosis: frameworks that rely on individual control "are not enough" [Prainsack et al. 2022, p. e773](pin:Prainsack-et-al-2022#p1 "regulatory frameworks that seek to prevent harm mostly by strengthening the control that individuals have over their data are not enough") ✓, because control "cannot address the vast power asymmetries between data processors and data subjects" [Prainsack et al. 2022, p. e773](pin:Prainsack-et-al-2022#p1 "Although individual control is important, it cannot address the vast power asymmetries between data processors and data subjects") ✓. Their answer has three parts [Prainsack et al. 2022, p. e773](pin:Prainsack-et-al-2022#p1 "It consists of three main pillars: facilitating good data use (pillar 1); preventing and mitigating harm (pillar 2); and returning profits to the public domain (pillar 3).") ✓:

- **Public value decides.** Data use with little public value and substantial risk "should be prohibited", while low-risk, beneficial use "should receive more public support" [Prainsack et al. 2022, p. e773](pin:Prainsack-et-al-2022#p1 "Data use that creates little or no public value but poses substantial risks should be prohibited, with fines severe enough to deter even powerful corporations from breaking the law, and effective enforcement") ✓.
- **Remedies for harm** that remains, e.g. "harm mitigation bodies" next to existing legal remedies [Prainsack et al. 2022, p. e773](pin:Prainsack-et-al-2022#p1 "One way to reach this goal is the creation of harm mitigation bodies at regional, national, or supranational level that would complement existing legal remedies.") ✓.
- **Profits back** to the people and communities that made them possible, by taxation or data commons [Prainsack et al. 2022, p. e774](pin:Prainsack-et-al-2022#p2 "Where commercial profits accrue from data use, some of these profits need to come back to the people and communities that enabled them in the first place.") ✓.

**The application to neural data.** Szoszkiewicz & Yuste offer data solidarity as the middle course between a market in neural data and a ban on sharing it: neural data as "a shared resource that serves the public good" [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3471](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p3 "A more balanced alternative—echoing the concept of data solidarity—would treat neural data as a shared resource that serves the public good (Prainsack et al, 2022).") ✓. They set it beside the medical-device model as a path that "both protects individual rights and unlocks neural data for socially beneficial purposes" [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3472](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p4 "data solidarity governance offers a path that both protects individual rights and unlocks neural data for socially beneficial purposes") ✓, and close by naming "user agency, solidarity and precaution" as the principles a legal framework should implement [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3472](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p4 "implement the principles of user agency, solidarity and precaution") ✓. Precaution may include "temporary moratoria on the most controversial neurotechnologies" (see [[moratorium]]) [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3472](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p4 "temporary moratoria on the most controversial neurotechnologies until longitudinal data accrue") ✓.

Read together: data solidarity answers a governance question (who decides which uses of data go ahead, and who gains), not the category question that [[is-neural-data-special]] asks (whether neural data need a legal class of their own).

## How NOT to read this

- Prainsack et al. 2022 is about health and digital data in general. It never mentions neurotechnology or criminal justice. The step to neural data is Szoszkiewicz & Yuste's, three years later.
- Both are (f) scholarship, written partly by the workshop's trainer. Neither is a statement of what international law requires.
- In custody, "power asymmetries" take a sharper form than between a company and its users. The library does not apply data solidarity there; [[consent-in-custody]] collects what it does say about that setting.

## Open questions

- Could data solidarity's public-value test work in the administration of justice, where the "public" interest is often the State's own interest in conviction?
- Who would sit on a "harm mitigation body" for neural data, and would it reach police and prison uses?
- The Secretary-General's recommendations (see [[what-should-states-do-now]]) speak of safeguards and prohibitions, not of collective governance: an ingest of A/HRC/57/61 may show whether the Advisory Committee goes further.
