# What does the library ask States to do now?

**Short answer.** Four documents make recommendations, and they ask different things of different addressees. The Secretary-General (c) asks Member States for ten measures: regulate, treat neural data as highly sensitive, prohibit coercive use, exclude tainted evidence, require demanding consent, validate evidence, protect vulnerable groups, respect Indigenous rights, hold business to account, and train. UNODC and INTERPOL (c) ask law enforcement and justice institutions not to deploy until accuracy and safety are demonstrated, and to build frameworks, oversight and harmonized standards. The Australian Human Rights Commission (d) asks its own Attorney-General for an inquiry with a moratorium meanwhile, and asks the Human Rights Committee for a General Comment on article 18. Futures Lab (e) asks the United Nations for new global standards and the United States for a federal framework. None is an obligation; each is a recommendation in its author's register.

## Reasoning, by register

**(c) The Secretary-General, to Member States.** The premise is that the law exists but needs clarifying [A/81/351, para. 48](pin:A/81/351#p15 "International human rights law provides an applicable framework to address the risks of neurotechnology, but further clarification and consistent application are needed") ✓, with a request that UN mechanisms provide guidance within their mandates. The recommendations ("I recommend that Member States") open with regulation [A/81/351, para. 50 (a)](pin:A/81/351#p15 "Establish regulatory frameworks, consistent with international human rights law and relevant international standards and guidance provided by United Nations human rights mechanisms, governing the development, deployment and use of neurotechnologies in the administration of justice") ✓; then data [A/81/351, para. 50 (b)](pin:A/81/351#p15 "Recognize neural data as highly sensitive personal data subject to the highest level of legal protection") ✓; a prohibition [A/81/351, para. 50 (c)](pin:A/81/351#p15 "Prohibit the compulsory or coercive use of neurotechnologies in the administration of justice, including brain-based deception detection, predictive applications, decoding applications to “read” thoughts, intentions or memories, neuromodulation and arbitrary or unlawful cognitive surveillance and monitoring") ✓; an exclusionary rule [A/81/351, para. 50 (d)](pin:A/81/351#p15 "Prohibit the use of neural data obtained through torture or ill-treatment, or in violation of the rights to freedom of thought and opinion, as evidence in any proceedings") ✓; consent [A/81/351, para. 50 (e)](pin:A/81/351#p15 "Require free, prior, informed, explicit and revocable consent for any potential use of neurotechnology in the administration of justice") ✓; validation of evidence [A/81/351, para. 50 (f)](pin:A/81/351#p15 "Require rigorous independent scientific review and validation of any neurotechnology-derived evidence proposed for admission in court") ✓; safeguards for groups [A/81/351, para. 50 (g)](pin:A/81/351#p16 "Ensure enhanced safeguards for children, detainees, persons with disabilities, women, older persons, persons belonging to particular racial or ethnic groups") ✓; Indigenous Peoples [A/81/351, para. 50 (h)](pin:A/81/351#p16 "Ensure that the development and deployment of neurotechnology respects the rights of Indigenous Peoples, including the requirement of free, prior and informed consent") ✓; business [A/81/351, para. 50 (i)](pin:A/81/351#p16 "Urge all business enterprises involved in neurotechnology to take all steps to meet their responsibility to respect human rights") ✓; and training [A/81/351, para. 50 (j)](pin:A/81/351#p16 "specialized training for law enforcement officials, prosecutors, judges and defence counsel") ✓.

**(c) UNODC and INTERPOL, to law enforcement and justice institutions.** A background paper, by its own account [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 3](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p6 "This background paper shares some preliminary thoughts on the field of neurotechnology and its significance for law enforcement and the criminal justice system") ✓, not a list of recommendations to States. Its first ask is a precondition [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 5](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p8 "Technologies should therefore not be deployed until their accuracy, safety, and feasibility have been rigorously demonstrated through independent testing and peer-reviewed research") ✓; then frameworks, consent, data protection and oversight [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 6](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p9 "establishing clear legal and ethical frameworks to protect human rights, ensuring voluntary and informed consent from individuals, implementing robust data protection and cybersecurity measures, and maintaining independent oversight") ✓; rules on evidence [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 6](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p9 "developing clear rules on admissibility of evidence, establishing independent mechanisms for oversight and accountability") ✓; and cooperation [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 6](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p9 "international cooperation and harmonization of standards will be essential to addressing regulatory gaps") ✓. Its next step is its own [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 6](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p9 "a Technology Assessment will be developed to provide more comprehensive guidance") ✓.

**(d) The Australian Human Rights Commission, to Australia and to the Human Rights Committee.** Recommendation 6 is addressed to the Attorney-General [AHRC 2025, p. 70](pin:AHRC-2025#p70 "refer to the Australian Law Reform Commission an inquiry to consider the potential application of neurotechnology in the criminal justice system, and examine in what circumstances (if any) it can be appropriately used. Until this work is completed there should be a moratorium on the use of neurotechnology in the criminal justice system") ✓, with an interim evidentiary position in the body text [AHRC 2025, p. 32](pin:AHRC-2025#p32 "until that time, information gained via neurotechnology should be inadmissible at trial") ✓. Recommendation 4 is addressed to a treaty body [AHRC 2025, p. 70](pin:AHRC-2025#p70 "The United Nations Human Rights Committee provide guidance on freedom of thought and neurotechnologies through the adoption of a general comment on article 18 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights") ✓. Both are an NHRI's recommendations; the moratorium is for one country's criminal justice system.

**(e) Futures Lab, to the United Nations and the United States.** [Futures Lab 2026, p. 2](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p3 "the United Nations should establish global standards grounded in human dignity, cognitive liberty, and equality") ✓, restated as new standards with monitoring meanwhile [Futures Lab 2026, p. 7](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p8 "the United Nations needs to develop new human rights standards that reflect the emerging and rapidly evolving nature of neurotechnology. Until such standards are established, the use of neurotechnology, particularly in legal responses to violations of neurorights, should be subject to careful monitoring") ✓; and, at home, [Futures Lab 2026, p. 8](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p9 "the United States must implement a federal framework grounded in human rights law using the EU model") ✓.

**Side by side.** All four want validation before use, consent, oversight and a rule on evidence. They differ on the pause: the Secretary-General prohibits coercive use and regulates the rest; UNODC and INTERPOL make deployment wait for demonstrated accuracy; the AHRC suspends all use in criminal justice pending an inquiry; Futures Lab monitors pending new standards. And they differ on where guidance should come from: existing mechanisms (A/81/351 para. 48; AHRC Recommendation 4, a General Comment) or new standards (Futures Lab). That second difference is the one on [[new-rights-vs-existing-rights]].

## What the library cannot answer

- What any State has done in response: the reports describe laws and rulings that predate them ([[regulatory-landscape]]).
- Whether the Human Rights Committee has taken up Recommendation 4, or whether the Advisory Committee's guidelines have been adopted (A/81/351 para. 46 reports the mandate only; [[a-hrc-57-61]] is not yet ingested).
- How a moratorium would be defined in law: the AHRC gives the scope ("criminal justice system", "criminal investigations") but no instrument.
