# Can a suspect validly consent to brain-based lie detection?

**Short answer.** No document in the library says consent in custody is impossible, and none says a consented test is lawful. Every document that addresses it treats such consent as suspect and conditions it: the Human Rights Committee's standard for article 14 (3) (g) reaches indirect and psychological pressure, not only force; the Secretary-General recommends prohibiting compulsory or coercive use and holding any consent to the highest evidentiary standards; UNODC and INTERPOL require consent free of undue influence, particularly in custody; the Australian Human Rights Commission doubts that consent given against an offer of leniency is voluntary and, for now, prefers a moratorium. The one ruling the library reports, Selvi, concerned involuntary administration, and the Secretary-General notes that voluntary use continued after it.

## Reasoning

**The standard (b).** The Committee reads the privilege against self-incrimination as a prohibition of pressure in all its forms [General Comment No. 32, para. 41](pin:CCPR/C/GC/32#p13 "This safeguard must be understood in terms of the absence of any direct or indirect physical or undue psychological pressure from the investigating authorities on the accused, with a view to obtaining a confession of guilt") ✓, and, where article 7 is in play, puts the burden on the State [General Comment No. 32, para. 41](pin:CCPR/C/GC/32#p13 "in such cases the burden is on the State to prove that statements made by the accused have been given of their own free will") ✓. The General Comment predates neurotechnology and says nothing about it; the applications below are the reports' own.

**The application (c).** The Secretary-General applies the standard to lie detection in terms of coercion [A/81/351, para. 32](pin:A/81/351#p10 "The coercive use of brain-based lie detection could call into question the privilege against self-incrimination, protected under article 14 (3) (g) of the Covenant. This privilege is an absolute guarantee and any direct or indirect physical or psychological pressure on the accused with a view to obtaining a confession of guilt is prohibited") ✓, and reports the Indian ruling on involuntary tests together with what followed it [A/81/351, para. 9](pin:A/81/351#p4 "The Supreme Court of India held that the involuntary administration of brain electrical oscillation signature profiling tests violates the constitutional protection against self-incrimination and renders the resulting evidence inadmissible. However, voluntary use appears to have continued in practice") ✓. On consent in custody the report speaks in the neuromodulation context, but in general terms [A/81/351, para. 29](pin:A/81/351#p10 "In custodial settings, the pronounced power imbalance between the State and the individual may call into question the genuineness of consent. Experts affirm that where such techniques are presented in connection with prospects such as early release or favourable parole outcomes, there is a significant risk that consent may not be fully free or voluntary") ✓. Its recommendations keep the two ideas apart: prohibit the coercive use [A/81/351, para. 50 (c)](pin:A/81/351#p15 "Prohibit the compulsory or coercive use of neurotechnologies in the administration of justice, including brain-based deception detection") ✓; for any other use, require consent of a demanding kind [A/81/351, para. 50 (e)](pin:A/81/351#p15 "Require free, prior, informed, explicit and revocable consent for any potential use of neurotechnology in the administration of justice, accompanied by full transparency regarding risks, access to independent legal counsel of one’s own choice and effective avenues for redress. Given the constrained and coercive nature of justice settings, any consent should be subject to the highest evidentiary standards and rigorous scrutiny") ✓. The Secretary-General does not say consent is impossible; he says it must be proved to a high standard.

UNODC and INTERPOL name the risk of the "offer" [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 4](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p7 "there is concern about the potential for neurotechnology to be used coercively, or under conditions in which individuals are “offered” the use of these technologies in ways that are coercive") ✓, set the condition [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 6](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p9 "Consent must be voluntary and informed, free of coercion or undue influence, particularly in custodial or correctional settings where power imbalances are acute") ✓, and leave the court-order question open [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 5](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p8 "Can courts legally order a criminal defendant, even in narrow circumstances, to cooperate with law enforcement in the use of neurotechnologies?") ✓.

**The inducement problem (d).** The Australian Human Rights Commission is the one document that works through an example [AHRC 2025, p. 32](pin:AHRC-2025#p32 "if an accused was offered the opportunity for a reduced sentence or some level of immunity in exchange for consenting to the use of neurotechnology during questioning by police, it would be difficult to argue that any consent given was entirely voluntary") ✓, and notes that pressure can come from refusal's consequences [AHRC 2025, p. 32](pin:AHRC-2025#p32 "indirect pressure, which may arise in informing an accused of the consequences of refusing to undergo questioning via neurotechnology") ✓. Its answer, for now, is not to refine consent but to pause [AHRC 2025, p. 32](pin:AHRC-2025#p32 "The Commission’s preliminary view is that a moratorium on the use of neurotechnology in criminal investigations should be imposed until the ALRC has published the findings of the recommended inquiry. Additionally, until that time, information gained via neurotechnology should be inadmissible at trial") ✓. This is an NHRI's preliminary view, addressed to Australia.

**A limit on what can be consented to (e).** Futures Lab would restrict the scope of consent until standards exist [Futures Lab 2026, p. 7](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p8 "Individuals should not be able to consent to overly broad uses of neurotechnology until clear and established neurorights are in place") ✓.

Read together: the higher the register, the more the answer is "consent is possible but must be shown to be free"; the lower, the more it is "not yet".

## What the library cannot answer

- Whether a suspect's consent, once given freely, makes the result admissible: A/81/351 ties inadmissibility to coercion and ill-treatment (para. 32), not to consent, and no document says a consented result is admissible.
- Whether the Committee's "undue psychological pressure" covers an offer of leniency: the General Comment does not say, and only the AHRC (d) argues it.
- What Selvi says about consented tests: the library reports the holding on involuntary administration only ([[selvi-v-state-of-karnataka]]).
- Whether a test the suspect asks for, to clear themselves, raises the same issues: no document addresses it.
