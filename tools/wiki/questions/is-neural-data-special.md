# Is neural data different from other personal data?

**Short answer.** On sensitivity, every register in the library says yes: the Secretary-General reports a "broad consensus" that neural data are highly sensitive and need heightened protection, and the UN agency paper, the NHRI, the civil-society submission and the scholarship say the same in their own words. The documents part on what follows. The laws they report (California, Colorado, Montana, Brazil) and the Secretary-General's recommendation put neural data inside an existing category, sensitive personal data, at its highest level. Szoszkiewicz and Yuste argue it must be treated as "fundamentally distinct", with a tailored framework. Prainsack and others answer a different question: governance should turn on public value and collective control, not on the category of the data.

## Reasoning

**Sensitive, by consensus (c).** [A/81/351, para. 5](pin:A/81/351#p3 "There is broad consensus that neural data are highly sensitive, providing unprecedented access to internal mental states, and that they require heightened ethical and legal protection") ✓ (its footnote 7, on the same PDF page, cites A/HRC/58/58, Szoszkiewicz and Yuste 2025, and Genser, Damianos and Yuste 2024; footnote 6 takes the definition from the UNESCO Recommendation). The report gives reasons [A/81/351, para. 25](pin:A/81/351#p9 "Neural data are uniquely revealing since they are capable of disclosing certain aspects of identity and personality") ✓ and [A/81/351, para. 25](pin:A/81/351#p9 "Neural data is biometric in character and may reportedly be re-identified across data sets and yield insights that were not foreseeable at the time of collection") ✓. UNODC and INTERPOL agree on the premise [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 4](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p7 "Brain activity is among the most intimate forms of personal information") ✓.

**What follows, for the Secretary-General: an existing category, at its top.** [A/81/351, para. 50 (b)](pin:A/81/351#p15 "Recognize neural data as highly sensitive personal data subject to the highest level of legal protection; impose clear limitations on its collection, processing, sale, storage, retention, reuse and sharing") ✓. Article 17 is the frame: the Committee's requirements for personal data [A/81/351, para. 26](pin:A/81/351#p9 "would also apply to neural data and other information retrieved through the use of neurotechnologies") ✓. This is existing law applied, with a justice-specific worry that consumer data may reach courts [A/81/351, para. 14](pin:A/81/351#p5 "without recognition of its uniquely sensitive character") ✓.

**The laws as reported (c, d, e, f): the same move.** Three US states, as the library reports them, added neural data to statutory definitions of sensitive data ([[california-neural-data-law]], [[colorado-neural-data-law]]) [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3471](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p3 "apply to these data existing consumer legislation for personal sensitive data") ✓; Brazil did likewise [A/81/351, para. 43](pin:A/81/351#p13 "In Brazil, neural data is classified as sensitive personal data") ✓. The AHRC frames the Australian question the same way [AHRC 2025, p. 23](pin:AHRC-2025#p23 "a key consideration will be whether neural data is classified as ‘personal information’ or ‘sensitive information’") ✓. Futures Lab wants more at the international level [Futures Lab 2026, p. 4](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p5 "specific international standards must be established to recognize the highly sensitive and personal nature of bio- and neurotechnologies") ✓.

**A category of its own (f).** Szoszkiewicz and Yuste go further than the laws they describe. The gap is that outside medicine the data are [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3471](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p3 "not classified as sensitive information when collected outside medical contexts by consumer products") ✓; the reason neural data differ is that they [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3470](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p2 "also reflect subconscious and involuntary activity, exposing information that individuals may not consciously recognize") ✓; and the conclusion is categorical [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3470](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p2 "Unlike conventional privacy, mental privacy demands a tailored regulatory framework. As it can reveal the most intimate aspects of a person, neural data must be treated as fundamentally distinct, with enhanced safeguards") ✓. Yet their proposal builds on data protection rather than replacing it [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3469](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p1 "We propose strengthening existing data protection regulations with a broad approach to safeguard neural data") ✓.

**A different answer: not category, governance (f).** Prainsack and others, writing about health data generally, argue that individual control is the wrong lever [Prainsack et al. 2022, p. e773](pin:Prainsack-et-al-2022#p1 "regulatory frameworks that seek to prevent harm mostly by strengthening the control that individuals have over their data are not enough") ✓ and that the test should be public value against risk [Prainsack et al. 2022, p. e773](pin:Prainsack-et-al-2022#p1 "Data use that creates little or no public value but poses substantial risks should be prohibited") ✓. On that view the question "is neural data special?" matters less than "what is this use for, and who bears the risk?". Szoszkiewicz and Yuste take the idea up as one option for neural data [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3471](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p3 "would treat neural data as a shared resource that serves the public good") ✓.

## What the library cannot answer

- Whether "highly sensitive personal data" in existing data-protection law gives the protection the Secretary-General and the scholarship call for: no document tests a statute against neural data.
- Whether inferences drawn from non-neural data count: the California definition excludes them, the UNESCO definition as quoted is silent, and Szoszkiewicz and Yuste report both practices ([[neural-data]]).
- Whether neural data obtained in criminal investigations falls under data-protection law at all, rather than criminal procedure: the US laws and the Chilean ruling, as reported, are consumer cases.
