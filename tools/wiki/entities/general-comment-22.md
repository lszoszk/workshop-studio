# General Comment No. 22 as an authority in the library

**What it is.** General Comment No. 22 (1993) of the Human Rights Committee on article 18 of the ICCPR, document CCPR/C/21/Rev.1/Add.4, register (b): treaty-body interpretation, not treaty text. [[ccpr-c-21-rev-1-add-4]] reads the document; this page records who in the library relies on it, and for what. It was written before neurotechnology was a question and does not mention it.

## In the library

**The paragraphs that travel.** Two. [General Comment No. 22, para. 1](pin:CCPR/C/21/Rev.1/Add.4#p1 "is far-reaching and profound; it encompasses freedom of thoughts on all matters") ✓, with non-derogability, [General Comment No. 22, para. 1](pin:CCPR/C/21/Rev.1/Add.4#p1 "this provision cannot be derogated from, even in time of public emergency") ✓; and [General Comment No. 22, para. 3](pin:CCPR/C/21/Rev.1/Add.4#p2 "It does not permit any limitations whatsoever on the freedom of thought and conscience") ✓, [General Comment No. 22, para. 3](pin:CCPR/C/21/Rev.1/Add.4#p2 "In accordance with articles 18 (2) and 17, no one can be compelled to reveal his thoughts") ✓.

**The Secretary-General (c)** relies on both, attributing them to "the Human Rights Committee" without naming the General Comment in the text: [A/81/351, para. 20](pin:A/81/351#p7 "The Human Rights Committee has affirmed that the freedom of thought, conscience and religion is “far-reaching and profound; it encompasses freedom of thought on all matters”, and that the freedom of thought and conscience “does not permit any limitations”") ✓ (footnote 36, on the same PDF page, cites General Comment No. 22, paras. 1 and 3), and [A/81/351, para. 21](pin:A/81/351#p7 "“no one can be compelled to reveal his thoughts”") ✓ (footnote 40 cites para. 3). What it draws from them is its own: that decoding and coercive neuromodulation [A/81/351, para. 20](pin:A/81/351#p7 "could thus have a profound impact on the freedom of thought") ✓. One small drift: the Committee wrote "thoughts on all matters"; the report writes "thought".

**The AHRC (d)** quotes the same two paragraphs, again without naming the General Comment in the text: [AHRC 2025, p. 28](pin:AHRC-2025#p28 "The right contained in art 18 ICCPR is ‘far reaching and profound’") ✓ (endnote 112, PDF p. 77, cites General Comment No. 22, para. 1) and [AHRC 2025, p. 39](pin:AHRC-2025#p39 "The right to freedom of thought is ‘far-reaching and profound; it encompasses freedom of thought on all matters’. As provided for under arts 18.2 and 17 ICCPR, ‘no one shall be compelled to reveal [their] thoughts’") ✓ (endnotes 248 and 249, PDF p. 81, cite paras. 1 and 3). The AHRC then asks for more than General Comment No. 22 gives: [AHRC 2025, p. 70](pin:AHRC-2025#p70 "the adoption of a general comment on article 18 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights") ✓ on freedom of thought and neurotechnologies.

**General Comment No. 34 (b)** is the only library document that names General Comment No. 22 in its text, and for a different point, the meaning of "morals": [General Comment No. 34, para. 32](pin:CCPR/C/GC/34#p8 "The Committee observed in general comment No. 22, that “the concept of morals derives from many social, philosophical and religious traditions") ✓.

**Finding.** No report in the library names General Comment No. 22 in its running text; two cite it in notes and carry its words as "the Human Rights Committee". UNODC–INTERPOL, the Futures Lab and the two journal articles do not use it. When a page needs the Committee's words, pin the General Comment, not the report that quotes it.
