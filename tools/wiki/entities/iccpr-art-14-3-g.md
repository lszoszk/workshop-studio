# ICCPR article 14(3)(g) (not to be compelled to testify against oneself)

**What it is.** Article 14(3) of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (1966), register (a), binding treaty text: "In the determination of any criminal charge against him, everyone shall be entitled to the following minimum guarantees, in full equality: … (g) Not to be compelled to testify against himself or to confess guilt." Source: [ICCPR art. 14(3)(g)](https://www.ohchr.org/en/instruments-mechanisms/instruments/international-covenant-civil-and-political-rights), (a) treaty text, not in the library. It is a criminal-charge guarantee, and the wrong it names is compulsion.

## In the library

**The Human Rights Committee (b).** General Comment No. 32 is the authority: [General Comment No. 32, para. 41](pin:CCPR/C/GC/32#p13 "This safeguard must be understood in terms of the absence of any direct or indirect physical or undue psychological pressure from the investigating authorities on the accused, with a view to obtaining a confession of guilt") ✓; statements obtained contrary to article 7 are excluded and [General Comment No. 32, para. 41](pin:CCPR/C/GC/32#p13 "the burden is on the State to prove that statements made by the accused have been given of their own free will") ✓; and [General Comment No. 32, para. 60](pin:CCPR/C/GC/32#p17 "to force them to make or sign, under duress, a confession admitting guilt violates both article 7 … and article 14, paragraph 3 (g)") ✓. See [[general-comment-32]] for how the reports reuse this formula.

**The Secretary-General (c).** [A/81/351, para. 32](pin:A/81/351#p10 "The coercive use of brain-based lie detection could call into question the privilege against self-incrimination, protected under article 14 (3) (g) of the Covenant. This privilege is an absolute guarantee") ✓ (footnote 62, on the same PDF page, cites General Comment No. 32). The report then records that the Supreme Court of India [A/81/351, para. 32](pin:A/81/351#p10 "held that the involuntary administration of brain electrical oscillation signature profiling tests, a form of brain-based lie detection, violates the constitutional protection against self-incrimination") ✓ (see [[selvi-v-state-of-karnataka]]). Earlier it lists the privilege among the [A/81/351, para. 9](pin:A/81/351#p4 "serious concerns regarding consent, coercion, reliability, and the privilege against self-incrimination") ✓ and notes that, despite that judgment, [A/81/351, para. 9](pin:A/81/351#p4 "voluntary use appears to have continued in practice") ✓.

**UNODC and INTERPOL (c).** Among the due process rights at stake: [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 4](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p7 "the presumption of innocence, the privilege against self-incrimination, and the right to appeal") ✓.

**The Australian Human Rights Commission (d).** [AHRC 2025, p. 31](pin:AHRC-2025#p31 "Under international human rights law all people charged with a criminal offence have a right not to be compelled to testify against themselves or to confess guilt. This means there can be no direct or indirect physical or psychological pressure to secure a confession or testimony") ✓ (endnotes 144 and 145, PDF p. 78, cite article 14(3)(g) and A/HRC/57/61, not General Comment No. 32); [AHRC 2025, p. 32](pin:AHRC-2025#p32 "This right is found in art 14.3(g) ICCPR") ✓. Its contribution is the inducement case: [AHRC 2025, p. 32](pin:AHRC-2025#p32 "if an accused was offered the opportunity for a reduced sentence or some level of immunity in exchange for consenting to the use of neurotechnology during questioning by police, it would be difficult to argue that any consent given was entirely voluntary") ✓. See [[consent-in-custody]].

**Scholarship (f).** Szoszkiewicz and Yuste raise the privilege for consumer wearables that reach the courtroom: [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3470](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p2 "underlining the urgent need to strengthen protections for the right against self-incrimination in the era of neurotechnology") ✓. Prainsack et al. and the Futures Lab submission (e) do not mention it.
