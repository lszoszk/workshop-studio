# ICCPR article 17 (privacy)

**What it is.** Article 17 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (1966), register (a), binding treaty text: "1. No one shall be subjected to arbitrary or unlawful interference with his privacy, family, home or correspondence, nor to unlawful attacks on his honour and reputation. 2. Everyone has the right to the protection of the law against such interference or attacks." Source: [ICCPR art. 17](https://www.ohchr.org/en/instruments-mechanisms/instruments/international-covenant-civil-and-political-rights), (a) treaty text, not in the library. The AHRC reproduces paragraph 1 in a text box [AHRC 2025, p. 21](pin:AHRC-2025#p21 "No one shall be subjected to arbitrary or unlawful interference with his privacy, family, home or correspondence") ✓.

## In the library

**The Human Rights Committee (b).** The Committee's reading of article 17, General Comment No. 16 (1988), is cited in A/81/351's footnotes 47 and 51 (PDF p. 9) but is not in the library. Two General Comments that are held touch the article: [General Comment No. 22, para. 3](pin:CCPR/C/21/Rev.1/Add.4#p2 "In accordance with articles 18 (2) and 17, no one can be compelled to reveal his thoughts or adherence to a religion or belief") ✓, and [General Comment No. 34, para. 18](pin:CCPR/C/GC/34#p4 "As the Committee observed in its general comment No. 16, regarding article 17 of the Covenant, every individual should have the right to ascertain in an intelligible form, whether, and if so, what personal data is stored in automatic data files, and for what purposes") ✓.

**The Secretary-General (c).** The fullest treatment. [A/81/351, para. 24](pin:A/81/351#p8 "Articles 17 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights and article 16 of the Convention on the Rights of the Child protect individuals from arbitrary or unlawful interference with their privacy. Neurotechnology introduces new threats to privacy by significantly increasing the level of possible interference") ✓, with the Committee's test for an arbitrary interference, [A/81/351, para. 24](pin:A/81/351#p8 "interpreted broadly to include elements of inappropriateness, injustice, lack of predictability and due process of law, as well as elements of reasonableness, necessity and proportionality") ✓ (footnote 47, on the same PDF page, cites General Comment No. 16). It extends the right to neural data: [A/81/351, para. 25](pin:A/81/351#p9 "the right to privacy protects personal data, communications and the integrity of private life. This protection would include neural data") ✓, and the Committee's requirements for personal data, [A/81/351, para. 26](pin:A/81/351#p9 "the gathering and holding of personal information must be regulated by law … These requirements would also apply to neural data") ✓. The extension is the Secretary-General's, not the Committee's.

**UNODC and INTERPOL (c).** Without an article number: [UNODC–INTERPOL 2025, p. 4](pin:UNODC-INTERPOL-2025#p7 "Brain activity is among the most intimate forms of personal information, and any attempt to monitor, decode or manipulate it … without genuine consent would represent a profound intrusion into human dignity, autonomy, and privacy") ✓.

**The Australian Human Rights Commission (d).** [AHRC 2025, p. 21](pin:AHRC-2025#p21 "The right to privacy is a cornerstone right that underpins freedoms of association, thought and expression") ✓; on memory recovery, [AHRC 2025, p. 33](pin:AHRC-2025#p33 "a real risk of breaching the right to privacy and freedom of thought as protected by arts 17 and 18 ICCPR") ✓; and, pairing the two articles as General Comment No. 22 does, [AHRC 2025, p. 39](pin:AHRC-2025#p39 "As provided for under arts 18.2 and 17 ICCPR, ‘no one shall be compelled to reveal [their] thoughts’") ✓. Its domestic question, whether neural data counts as personal or sensitive information under Australia's Privacy Act, [AHRC 2025, p. 25](pin:AHRC-2025#p25 "needs to be clarified to ensure the human right to privacy is not diminished by legislative uncertainty") ✓, does not travel.

**Civil society (e).** The Futures Lab cites the Universal Declaration, not the Covenant: [Futures Lab 2026, p. 4](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p5 "Articles 12 and 18 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR) which protect privacy and freedom of thought, conscience, and religion") ✓.

**Scholarship (f).** Szoszkiewicz and Yuste do not name article 17; their claim is that [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3470](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p2 "Unlike conventional privacy, mental privacy demands a tailored regulatory framework") ✓. Prainsack et al. do not mention privacy.
