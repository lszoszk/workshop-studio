# Chile Supreme Court, 2023 (neural data, Emotiv)

**What it is.** A 2023 ruling of the Supreme Court of Chile on a consumer brain-monitoring device, applying the country's 2021 constitutional amendment on brain activity and the data derived from it. A/81/351 names it in a footnote as "Supreme Court of Chile, Girardi v. Emotiv Inc., Judgment, 9 August 2023" (footnote 75, PDF p. 13); Futures Lab 2026 calls it Girardi v. Emotiv Inc. in its text. It is domestic law, reported by three library documents of registers (c), (e) and (f). The judgment itself is not in the library, and this page carries the registers of the reports.

## In the library

The Secretary-General gives the holding in one sentence and its significance in another [A/81/351, para. 43](pin:A/81/351#p13 "In Chile, in 2023, the Supreme Court applied the 2021 constitutional amendment, to protect brain activity and derived data, holding unlawful a company’s practices involving a portable brain-monitoring device. The ruling was among the first globally to address neural data under the rights to mental privacy and physical and mental integrity") ✓. Footnote 75, on the same PDF page, names the amending law (Act No. 21.383 of 2021, amending article 19 of the Constitution) and the judgment.

Futures Lab, a civil-society submission, is the only document that gives facts. The purchaser was a senator, and the dispute was about access to one's own data [Futures Lab 2026, p. 6](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p7 "Senator Guido Girardi Lavin purchased a device from U.S.-based company Emotiv and later found that the terms of service restricted non-subscribers’ access to their neural data") ✓; the remedy went beyond the data [Futures Lab 2026, p. 6](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p7 "ordering the deletion of collected neural data and the suspension of sales until public health certification is obtained") ✓. The submission reads the case as a lesson about consent and prior assessment [Futures Lab 2026, p. 2](pin:FuturesLab-2026#p3 "demonstrates that commercialization cannot rely solely on click-through consent") ✓.

Szoszkiewicz and Yuste, in a journal article, add that the ruling was unanimous and tie it to the amendment's wording [Szoszkiewicz & Yuste 2025, p. 3471](pin:Szoszkiewicz-Yuste-2025#p3 "Chile’s pioneering 2021 constitutional amendment, which protects “cerebral activity and the information drawn from it”—that is, brain data—as a constitutional right. This amendment led to a 2023 unanimous ruling by Chile’s Supreme Court ordering a company to delete a consumer’s neural data, as its collection violated mental privacy protections") ✓.

The Australian Human Rights Commission mentions the amendment but not the ruling [AHRC 2025, p. 25](pin:AHRC-2025#p25 "Chile has worked to introduce neural protections into its national legal system via constitutional amendment") ✓.

**Where the reports differ.** They agree on the year, the court, the device and the deletion order. They differ in what they add. A/81/351 (c) states the legal ground (the amendment; mental privacy and physical and mental integrity) and no order beyond "unlawful". Futures Lab (e) gives the facts and a second order, the suspension of sales pending public health certification, which no other document mentions. Szoszkiewicz and Yuste (f) add "unanimous" and frame the ground as mental privacy alone. None quotes the judgment. The library cannot say which account is complete.

**What the library does not say.** The Court's reasoning; the text of the constitutional provision beyond the phrase Szoszkiewicz and Yuste quote; whether the case bears on the administration of justice at all, since every report places it in the consumer market. Read it as a neural-data case, not a criminal-justice one.
